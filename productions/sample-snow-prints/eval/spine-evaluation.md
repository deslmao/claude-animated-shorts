# Independent evaluation: One Set of Footprints
packet: 7626b28

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E3, E5, E6, E7, E9
- evidence: The hook picture works with no words: E1 shows one clean trail from the dark trees to the porch, with none leading away. Each clue after that is one physical object in its own shot: the door swinging open with a flash of the clock at 10:02 (E3), a palm on the dry mat (E5), the star in the snow and the star on the empty boot box (E6), the pillows (E9). At every point the viewer knows who is involved (you and Ivy), where (house, yard, woods) and what's at stake (an intruder near your sister, then your sister alone in the woods).
- problem: The one heavy beat is E7. The viewer has to invert a direction ("the prints came in, but the boots went out") from a split frame, and the packet doesn't make clear whether the whole rack is empty or only your slot (E6 says "the boot rack is empty" and also "the empty slot on the rack"). Neither breaks clarity, but see consistency.
- fix: (minor) Show the rack with your slot empty and Ivy's small boots still on it, so E7 reads as "my boots, specifically, went out".
### cause and effect
- result: weak
- beats: E3, E4, E7, E8, E9, E12
- evidence: The main chain is causal. The unlocked door leads to the search (E3→E4), the dry mat leads to a closer look (E5→E6), the boots lead to the deduction (E6→E7), the empty bed leads to the chase (E9→E10), the snow erases the trail and the drawing leads to the shed (E10→E11). But three links are driven by convenience, not by the characters. (1) E3: believing an intruder is inside at 2 a.m., with a seven-year-old upstairs, you search alone with an umbrella and never try anyone but Mum. (2) E7→E8: you've just concluded that "whoever made them is wearing your boots, outside", and instead of running to Ivy you stop on the porch to test footprints with your own foot. (3) E12: Ivy explains she walked backwards "so you'd search the house first", which means she deliberately made you think a burglar had come in. That's either cruel or illogical for a child trying not to be caught.
- problem: The protagonist's actions don't follow from "keep Ivy safe", the stated want, and Ivy's motive for the central trick is muddled. The top comments would pick at exactly these.
- fix: (1) Add a call to emergency services (see plot hole). (2) Merge E8 into E10: run to Ivy first (E9), then, as you follow the trail, step beside a print and see the deep toe. The test confirms the trick on the move, with no stop. (3) Restate Ivy's motive as protecting the fox's hiding place: tracks that point at the house send anyone following them to the house, not the shed. She copied the trick from her book and never thought it would look like a burglar. Her not realising becomes the emotional beat.
### emotional investment
- result: weak
- beats: E2, E9, E11, E12, E13
- evidence: Ivy is only ever a lump under a blanket, crayon foxes and a book (E2) until she's found (E12). We never see her awake, hear her, or see the sibling relationship. The danger to her is mostly implied: snow, night, a "wild fox", and a single cough (E11). The emotional payoff, "you wrap her in your coat instead of shouting" (E12), only lands if we have seen that you would normally shout, or that she's stubborn with you.
- problem: The stakes are generic ("little sister in danger"). The viewer cares in principle, not about this particular Ivy, so the reunion and the dawn image (E13) are pleasant rather than moving.
- fix: Plant one waking moment before the night: a 2–3 s bedtime flash in E2, where Ivy asks about the fox and you snap "No woods. Go to sleep", or something similar. It shows her stubbornness and your friction, and pays off when you choose the coat over shouting. Make the danger visible on the chase: a small mitten half-filled with snow, or her short prints stumbling.
### setup and payoff
- result: weak
- beats: E2, E6, E9, E11, E12, E13
- evidence: Strong payoffs: the crayon shed (E2) leads you to the shed (E11); the fox drawings and the NO WOODS note (E2) explain the fox (E12); the lump (E2) is pillows (E9); the E1 window angle returns at E13 with two trails, which neatly mirrors and recontextualises the opening image. Weaker: the star boots are introduced only at the moment of discovery (E6), and the cat-food tin (E9) and your scarf (E12) are never planted (no cat is shown). The biggest problem is E2: "a book, JUNIOR DETECTIVE: TRACKS AND TRICKS, open on the floor" beside "a lump under the blanket" in the same doorway shot.
- problem: The decoy-pillow trope is very familiar, and a readable tracks-and-tricks title next to a lingering lump announces the solution in the second beat. Savvy viewers will solve it at E2 and spend E3–E8 waiting.
- fix: In E2, keep the book closed or its title unreadable (a magnifying-glass cover), and don't hold on the lump. Save the readable page "FAKE TRACKS: WALK BACKWARDS" for E9. Plant your star boots on the rack in the E3 lock-up flash at 10:02. Plant the tin (a cat bowl in the kitchen, or the pantry cupboard; see dilemma balance).
### originality and adaptation
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E8, E9, E12
- evidence: The declared sources are used as techniques, not sequences. The Shining's backward-in-your-own-prints escape becomes a child faking an arrival to hide a departure. "The dog that didn't bark" becomes the dry mat and the missing boots. The pillow decoy is a generic trope. None of the benchmarks' distinctive expression appears: no duplicated or unreachable-by-text mum device, no rules list, no self-authored clues, no countdown doors. The resolution (a child secretly nursing an injured wild animal) is a familiar family-film trope, but it's paired with an original clue chain, and it works fully for someone who knows none of the sources.
- problem: (none blocking) The "secret injured animal" ending is the most familiar part.
- fix: Optional: give the fox reveal one specific detail of this story's own, for example a bandage made from your scarf, or the backward prints taken from a page in her book about how foxes hide their tracks, so the trick and the animal connect.
### accuracy
- result: n/a fiction
- beats: E8, E10, E13
- evidence: Fiction. Its one physical mechanism, backward walking giving a shorter stride and toe-deep prints, is broadly consistent with tracking lore. Fresh snow filling prints "within minutes" is plausible in heavy snowfall.
- problem: (comment risk, not a factual error) Carrying an injured wild fox home in a box (E13) invites "never touch a wild fox" comments, and the story's own NO WOODS rule cites the fox as dangerous.
- fix: Optional: end with the fox boxed for the vet or wildlife rescue (Mum works at the hospital: a call at dawn), rather than taken in as a pet.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the story has an intruder scare at 2 a.m. and then a missing seven-year-old in snowy woods
- result: fail
- beats: E3, E4, E9, E10, E12, E13
- finding: Top-comment probe: "Why didn't you just call the police?" The rules close off only Mum (her phone is in her locker). At E3 you believe a stranger is in the house with your little sister, and at E9 a seven-year-old is gone into the woods at night as snow starts. In both cases calling emergency services, or a neighbour, is the obvious move, and the story never shows it tried or failed. Secondary holes: (a) Ivy's stated motive ("so you'd search the house first") makes her deliberately frighten you, which viewers will question. (b) At E7 you suspect someone in your boots is outside, yet you test footprints (E8) before checking on Ivy. (c) E12 is about 2:30 a.m. and E13 is "at dawn": why stay hours in a cold shed with a coughing child? And the trails home at dawn need the snow to have stopped. (d) Cat food with no cat. Fix: make the call a visible beat. At E9, run out dialling, and a dispatcher says the roads are blocked by snow and help is far away. You're the only one who can follow before the trail fills. That turns the hole into the stake that drives E10. Fix (a)–(c) as in cause and effect. For (c), cut "dawn" (walk home under a clearing sky) or show the snow stopping.
### consistency
- applies: yes, the story uses a timeline (10:02 lock, midnight stop, 2 a.m. restart, dawn) and stated rules (the star boots, the backward-walking signature, NO WOODS)
- result: weak
- beats: E1, E6, E8, E10, E13
- finding: (1) E6: "the boot rack is empty" contradicts "the empty slot on the rack". If the whole rack is empty, Ivy's own boots are also gone and the star logic weakens. (2) E8: your backward print matches "exactly", including "the same short stride". An older sibling's backward stride shouldn't match a seven-year-old's. The rule names toe depth as the signature, so match that and let her stride be shorter still, which is a fair clue that the walker was small. (3) The timeline holds (snow stops at 0:00, Ivy leaves after it, you wake at 2:00, snow resumes), but E13 at dawn implies a long, unexplained wait and needs the snow to have stopped. (4) The rule "the woods are forbidden because there's a wild fox" ends with the fox carried into the house; acknowledge it (Mum or the vet) or the rule reads as dropped. (5) The tin and scarf aren't in any earlier beat.
### dilemma balance
- applies: yes, the premise sets two explanations (A: an intruder came out of the woods and is hiding inside; B: nobody came in, and the prints were made by someone leaving)
- result: weak
- beats: E1–E9
- finding: For A: the trail pointing in with none out (E1), the unlocked door (E3), the thump (E4, debunked in the same beat as falling snow). For B: the dry mat (E5), the star tread plus your empty slot (E6), the deduction (E7), the toe-deep test (E8), plus the E2 telegraph (a lump and a readable TRACKS AND TRICKS title). A gains no new evidence after E3 and is effectively dead at E5, about 40% of the way in. Meanwhile B, specifically "it's Ivy", is visible from E2 to anyone who knows the pillow trope. So A is plausible only briefly, and the real answer is guessable early. Fix: give A one fresh, misleading piece of evidence during the E4 search that Ivy actually caused, for example the pantry cupboard left open with a gap where a tin was, or your scarf gone from its hook. It reads as an intruder rummaging and is recontextualised at E9 (the missing cat-food tin) and E12 (the scarf on the fox). Combined with hiding the book title at E2, this keeps both explanations alive until E5–E6.
### emotional cost
- applies: yes, the protagonist can lose Ivy
- result: weak
- beats: E2, E9, E10, E11
- finding: The stated loss is Ivy, and the snow erasing the only trail. The clock is shown well (E10: snowflakes filling each print). The danger to Ivy herself isn't shown before the climax, only a cough at E11, and we never see her as a person before she's missing, so the cost is understood rather than felt. Fix: the bedtime flash (emotional investment), plus one visible sign of her vulnerability on the trail (a dropped mitten filling with snow, a stumble in the oversized boots).
### reproduction
- applies: yes, inspirations are declared (The Shining, Sherlock Holmes, the pillow-decoy trope)
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E7, E8, E9, E12
- finding: Closest source: The Shining (a boy steps backwards in his own prints in the snowy hedge maze to escape his father). Sequence: different; here a sibling reads a domestic mystery from clues in the house. Motivations: escaping a killer vs. a child hiding a secret rescue. Conflict: survival vs. misreading evidence. Solution: the backward-step trick is the clue to be decoded, not the escape. Payoff meaning: a reunion and protection, not escape from a monster. Only the technique is shared. "The dog that didn't bark" is used as a concept (the dry mat). Holmes's "Priory School" (disguised tracks, a missing child) shares only trope-level ideas. No benchmark's distinctive expression is reused, and the duplicated-mum device the benchmarks warn about is avoided. This is transformation, not reproduction.
### cold viewer
- applies: yes, always
- result: weak
- beats: E1–E13
- finding: Summary: "Footprints seemed to show someone coming out of the woods into my house, but the clues (dry mat, my missing star boots) showed the prints were made by someone walking backwards out of the house. It was my little sister, who'd sneaked into the forbidden woods in my boots to look after an injured fox in the old shed, and I found her before the snow covered the trail." The core is clear and satisfying. Likely misreadings or reactions: (1) many will think "pillows under the blanket, obviously" at E2; (2) "why didn't they call the police?"; (3) "why would she want her sibling to think there's a burglar?"; (4) a seven-year-old's stride "matching exactly" an older sibling's backward stride.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes. E1 is a strong, instantly legible hook (one line of prints in, none out), and the snow-filling clock (E10) and the shed reveal pull to the end. The risk points are E2, where trope-savvy viewers feel ahead of the story because of the readable book title and the lingering lump, and the E5–E8 clue-reading plateau, where danger drops and the protagonist stops to test prints instead of running to Ivy. A viewer is most likely to leave around E8.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Close the "just call the police" hole with one visible beat. At E9, run out dialling: help is far away on snowed-in roads, and you're the only one who can follow before the trail fills. Merge the E8 foot test into the chase (E10), so you never stop to test before checking on Ivy, and match toe depth, not stride (her shorter stride shows a small walker). 2. Keep both explanations alive longer. Hide the answer at E2 (book title unreadable until E9, no lingering on the lump), and give the intruder theory one misleading clue in the E4 search that Ivy caused (the pantry open and a tin gone, your scarf missing). E9 and E12 recontextualise it. Plant your star boots on the rack in the 10:02 flash, and show Ivy's small boots still there at E6. 3. Make Ivy a person and her trick a child's logic. Add a 2–3 s bedtime flash (she pleads about the fox; you snap "No woods"), so wrapping her in your coat instead of shouting pays it off. Restate her motive as hiding the fox (tracks pointing at the house send anyone following to the house, not the shed), and have her not realise it would look like a burglar. Fix the dawn gap, and end with the fox going to a vet or rescue rather than being kept.
