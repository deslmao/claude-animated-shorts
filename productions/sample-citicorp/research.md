# Research: the Citicorp Center engineering crisis (1978)

**Verified on 13 Nov 2026 against primary and secondary sources read in full** (not search snippets):
- **[M]** Joe Morgenstern, "The Fifty-Nine-Story Crisis", *The New Yorker*, 29 May 1995, pp. 45–53. Full text, as hosted by Duke University: https://people.duke.edu/~hpgavin/cee421/citicorp1.htm
- **[N]** Dat Duthinh, "Blown Away: Revisiting a Famous Engineering Case", NIST *Taking Measure* blog, 23 July 2019: https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/blown-away-revisiting-famous-engineering-case
- **[W]** Wikipedia, "Citicorp Center engineering crisis" (cross-checked; its claims cite their own sources): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citicorp_Center_engineering_crisis

## Facts used
| Claim in the script | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| The tower stood on four columns at the middle of each side, because St. Peter's Church owned the corner | M, W | M: the church's old building would be replaced by a new one, free-standing within the complex. |
| The tuned mass damper is a block of about 410 tons of concrete on springs, and it needs electric current | M | The script says only "the block". |
| The wind braces are V-shaped (chevrons), and the design was checked against winds on the faces | M, N | N: the 1970 code required face (head-on) winds only. |
| In **May** 1978, at a meeting about Pittsburgh towers, he learned that the Citicorp joints had been bolted instead of welded | M | Bethlehem Steel proposed it; his New York office accepted it on 1 Aug 1974; it was cheaper. |
| In **June** 1978, a student's question about corner (quartering) winds prompted his re-check | M, N, W | Accounts differ. M (1995): a male student, name "lost", phoned LeMessurier. W: Princeton student Diane Hartley dealt with junior staff member Joel Weinstein. The script says "a student asked his firm a question", which fits both. |
| Quartering winds raised the strain by 40% in four of the eight chevrons in each tier; with the bolted joints, he concluded the joints were the weak point | M | |
| By his estimate, a storm able to tear the critical joint apart was a "sixteen-year storm", "one chance in sixteen in any year, including that one"; with the damper running, one in fifty-five | M | The script attributes it to "his own maths". |
| He told the owners, aware it could mean "protracted litigation, probable bankruptcy, and professional disgrace" | M | The script says "could finish his career". |
| Plywood enclosures around the joints; welders from 8 p.m. to 4 a.m. with the fire-alarm system shut off; cleaned up before the first staff arrived | M | |
| Steel plates welded over the bolted joints | M, W | |
| Emergency generators for the damper; weather forecasters engaged; strain gauges installed | M, W | |
| Evacuation plans for the surrounding area were drawn up and not publicised | M, W | |
| Hurricane Ella was off Cape Hatteras heading for New York on 1 Sept 1978, with the repairs half done; it turned out to sea | M, N, W | |
| A newspaper strike shut the city's major papers; welding finished in October, several weeks before most papers resumed | M, W | W: officials described the work as routine. |
| The story became widely known through the 1995 New Yorker article | W | W: "not publicized until 1995". |
| NIST (Duthinh) concluded that face winds, not corner winds, govern the design; "if the building had been safely designed for head-on winds, then it would have been safe for quartering winds, regardless of the connection details"; "the rationale for retrofitting the building needs to be re-examined" | N | The script says "the corners may never have been the threat" (hedged), and that modern tools weren't available in 1978. N notes the designers didn't have today's tools. |

## Uncertain or not used
- **Who the student spoke to:** accounts differ, so the script is neutral.
- **The damper's exact tonnage:** sources vary (400–410 tons), so it isn't used.
- **LeMessurier's dialogue:** not quoted.
- **A "secret kept for years by the strike":** **not** claimed. The strike only covered the repair period.
