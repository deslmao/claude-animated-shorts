# Thinkora Winning Patterns

Techniques taken from the Shorts the owner analysed (17 Nov 2026). **Record the technique, never copy the story.** Add each new video you study using the template at the end.

## The rules every Thinkora Short follows
1. **The premise in the first sentence, as a picture.**
   - "Imagine your bus driver skips your stop…"
   - "My crush controls the weather."
   - "Gambling is illegal in Japan. Yet…"
   - The viewer knows the situation and the strangeness before second 3.
2. **Second person or first person, never a distant third person.** "You", or "I" for a character's life story. The viewer is inside it.
3. **A narrator explains every clue the moment it appears.**
   - "One of them is broken, just like Mom's car."
   - Nothing important is left for the viewer to decode alone.
   - Part 1 v1 failed exactly here.
4. **Words over every second.** Continuous narration, with short punchy sentences. Silence only as a deliberate beat of a second or less.
5. **Rules and stakes, stated.** "Rule 1, never turn on a light." Simple, numbered and remembered.
6. **Escalation every 5–10 seconds.** Each beat makes it worse or stranger (bus: the text → the car behind → Mom at the wheel → the headcount says two).
7. **One concrete, impossible detail per reveal.**
   - The headcount says two.
   - The photo of you asleep, taken from your room.
   - Ours: the flood that stops at the fence.
8. **End on a choice the viewer can answer.**
   - "So who do you trust?"
   - "Teeth or sheets?"
   - "Open the case or leave it shut?"
   - This drives comments, and the next part opens on the result ("Most of you said stay…").
9. **The next part recaps in one line, then pays off the vote.**
10. **Length follows the story.** About 60–90 s for horror POV. Longer story Shorts (2–2.5 min: the ship, the numbers) work only with nonstop narration and a clear goal.

**Lessons, matched against the scripts:**
1. **A complete, narrated mystery with a concrete impossible number wins.**
   - *One Too Many*: the premise in the first sentence, then a count the viewer joins in ("twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty… thirty-one?").
   - Retention stayed above 90% through 0:15.
2. **Silence kills.**
   - *Extra Door* had a 10-second wordless stretch at 15.7–25.7 s. Retention fell from 73% to 55% across it, and there were more silent stretches of 7–9 s.
   - Keep any gap under about 1.5 s unless it's filled with action and sound.
3. **Don't pause after answering the question.**
   - *One Too Many* lost 6% in 2 s at 1:16–1:18, right after the mystery was explained ("Nobody checked hers."), across a 1.4 s pause before the next act.
   - Hand each answer straight to the next question.
4. **A standalone call to action makes people leave.**
   - *Extra Door* fell from 29.7% to 13.4% during "Comment A or B. Your vote decides Part 2."
   - End on the dramatic question itself, with the vote on screen, and cut within about 0.5 s.
5. **Make the ending loop to the start.** *One Too Many* starts at 158%, from replays; its last line echoes its first.
6. **The audience:**
   - 95% arrives from the Shorts feed, so the first frame and first sentence decide everything.
   - Ages: 35–44 is 29%, 13–17 is 23%, 25–34 is 22%.
   - Countries: US 38%, Philippines 12%, India 7%, Australia 7%, UK 5%.
7. **Titles:** the concrete impossible-detail pattern ("She Counted 31 Pupils. The Register Said 30.").
8. **One story, one question, answered in the same Short** (owner feedback, 17 Nov 2026: *What Ollie Says*, Part 1
   drew "What is it about though?").
   - Part 1 stacked several mysteries (a language, a dead son, a flood, sleepwalking, a magic garden) and answered none
     of them, so viewers couldn't say what it was about.
   - The test before production: can the whole premise be said in one sentence a stranger understands? (*4,012 Steps*:
     "Your dead dog's collar still counts his steps round your bed every night.")
   - A series part still needs its own complete answer; the cliffhanger is a new question, not the old one left open.
9. **The first frame decides the swipe, before any word lands** (*4,012 Steps*, first hour: 27.7% stayed, 72.3%
   swiped, against *One Too Many*'s 85.5%).
   - *4,012 Steps* opened on a flat white phone screen: no face, no motion, no darkness. In a horror feed it reads as a
     screenshot or an ad, and viewers swipe before "Imagine…" finishes.
   - The rule from now on: frame 1 is a character with a strong expression, in the story's world, moving within the
     first 0.5 s, high contrast, with sound on frame 1. UI cards and text screens come second, never first.
   - Check it before rendering: view frame 1 at about 180 px wide (feed size) next to *One Too Many*'s frame 1.
   - Post at most one Short a day. Two uploads 30 minutes apart compete for the same test audience.

- **Explainers answer their own hook at once,** so there's no reason to stay, even with no silence.
- **Stories with good hooks lose people in their silences** (Whale City, Float Away, Extra Door).
- **Fragment hooks lose people at once** ("A car ride. Rat poison. A nap."; "…six… seven… …eight?").
- **Script gate for every new video:**
  - the first sentence is a complete, concrete, impossible situation;
  - its question isn't answered until late;
  - narration at about 150 words a minute;
  - 20% silent or less;
  - no gap over 2 s unless it's action with sound;
  - an ending that loops back to the start.

**Instagram (17 Nov 2026):**
- **Views are flat:** every reel sits at about 1,400–2,400, so Instagram isn't separating hits from misses yet.
- **Engagement does separate them:**
  - *One Too Many*: 118 interactions; Whale City: 88; Float Away: 83.
  - *The Extra Door*: **9 comments**, the most of any reel, against 7 on YouTube's 21k-view *One Too Many*.
  - The vote drives comments, and the story drives watch time. Use both.
- **Reel covers are random frames,** several caught mid-caption ("over here!", "heat", "a room and"). Every reel needs a deliberate cover: the hook image plus 3–5 words of title.
- **Instagram rewards sends,** so add a share prompt to the caption ("Send this to the person who always…", as the train series does).

## Hook types that worked
| Type | Example | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| "Imagine…" scenario | bus driver, 3:07 a.m. phone, fire lookout | The viewer is cast in the role at once |
| Diabolical act, explained later | "He put a bronze ball in a dragon's mouth…", "He sawed gaps in a railroad…" | "Why would he do that?" holds you until the payoff |
| Paradox fact | "It's impossible to get fired in Japan, but…" | The contradiction demands an answer |
| World rule | "In this world, everybody lives on one giant ship…" | A big premise in one sentence |
| Countdown or goal | "30 days until his reunion." | A clear finish line |

## Visual and sound techniques
- **Camera:** zoom out to establish, push in to the detail as the narrator names it (Rick's Clips: the table, then the apple).
- **Sound carries the fear:** panting, flickering lights, silence before a knock (the train series).
- **One clear readable image per line.** The picture must show what the sentence says, at phone size.
- **Captions on every word.**

## Comment drivers seen
- A binary vote ("1 or 2").
- A detail viewers can "solve" ("Rewatch 0:11 and look at the glass").
- Something slightly wrong that people argue about. Rick's Clips' "he forgot to explain" thread got 110k likes. Controversy over clarity helps, but confusion over the core story doesn't.

## Update, 20 Nov 2026: the owner's second research pass, and what our recent videos taught us
**New from the research (added to what's above):**
- **Series packaging:**
  - label parts "Night 1/7", "Day 1 of 30" or "Part 1";
  - each part ends on a binary choice, and **"the most-liked answer decides what happens next"**;
  - Part 2 opens with the result ("Most of you said stay…"), which catches up new viewers and rewards the voters.
- **Our own pinned comment on every video:** a rewatch clue with a timestamp, plus the 1-or-2 question.
- **Simple art is no handicap:** stickman and blocky-character channels get 2–9M views. The owner's verdict: "Thinkora has better animation and camera than most of these; their storytelling is better." Story and packaging are where we're behind.

**Originality and monetization (YouTube):**
- Formats can repeat; plots, rule sets and reveals can't. Interchangeable template stories are a monetization risk.
- Never copy someone else's intellectual property. Learn the technique, invent the world.
- Keep horror advertiser-friendly: dread, not gore, and nothing aimed at frightening young children.
- AI disclosure on YouTube is only required for realistic-looking content. On TikTok and Instagram, always label AI.
- The owner is under 18, so a parent or guardian handles AdSense, shop and payment accounts.

**What our recent videos taught us, against these rules:**
- **Stop Time Part 1** (231 views, 25.6% viewed):
  - an invented sci-fi world;
  - about 20 s of set-up before the real hook (the knock);
  - "Part 1" in the title;
  - a 9-second vote card at the end. Lesson 4 above already said: end on the question and cut within about 0.5 s.
- **Nova** (928 views, 44.8%): a premise that needed explaining, and an invented world.
- **The memories video:**
  - frame 1 is a glowing vial card (a face inside it, but not a character moving), which bends rule 9;
  - it runs 2:11;
  - judge it on "stayed to watch" at 48 h before making more sci-fi.

**The channel's format from now on:**
- **The story:** an "Imagine…" POV mystery in an everyday place (home, school, a bus, a train). A short set of rules, two voices the viewer trusts that contradict each other, one planted clue, an escalation every 5–10 s.
- **Frame 1:** a character with a strong expression, moving.
- **The ending:** a binary choice, cut fast, with the pinned comment carrying the vote. The most-liked answer decides Night 2.
- **Length:** 60–90 s.
