# Next story selection: five premises (for the owner's choice)

2026-11-15. Benchmark: **One Too Many** (31 Pupils). No scripts, assets or renders until a premise is chosen.

## What One Too Many does right
1. **The opening is a fact that's wrong:** "Every fire drill, Year Seven comes out with one pupil too many."
   - The audience has the question in one line.
   - The rule is a number you can see on screen.
2. **One rule, with no extra mechanics.** The count is off by one, and every later beat reuses that same rule.
3. **Each discovery changes what the rule means:**
   - the photo shows who the extra pupil is;
   - the plaque shows why she's there;
   - "a wrong count makes teachers check every face" explains why it's an extra pupil, not a missing one;
   - in the real fire, the count is right, and that is the horror.
4. **The hero acts and pays for it.** Ms Okoye does the thing nobody did in 1979: she checks every face and goes back in.
5. **The ending is a callback to the opening that needs no explanation.** The count says thirty-two.

## What The Other Jamie taught us
- **We never got to care about the person we were asked to save.**
  - The real Jamie is introduced as a missing person, then revealed as a liar who hit a girl.
  - The copy is someone we've known for about 20 seconds.
  - The choice was between two versions of a character we never got to know, so neither loss hurts.
- **There were too many rules:** the deal, the mirror trap, the swap, the clock, and "no third option". Each one had to be explained in voiceover instead of shown. One Too Many has one rule.
- **The hero is passive.** Sam mostly receives information. He never acts or pays a price, and in the end he hands the decision to the viewer.
- **The A/B ending replaced a payoff.** Asking the viewer to choose works only when both answers already hurt. Without that, it feels like the story stopped.

**The test for every premise below:**
- a one-line hook that is a visible oddity;
- one rule;
- a person we like within 10 seconds;
- a hero who does something that costs them;
- an ending image that pays off the opening.

## 1. The Painting That Goes Home
1. **Title:** The Painting That Goes Home
2. **Concept:** For 31 years, a museum cleaner has secretly carried the same painting home every night and hung it back before dawn, and the new night guard is about to catch her.
3. **Opening hook:**
   - 2:00 a.m., an empty gallery. An old cleaner lifts a painting off the wall, wraps it in her coat and walks out of the museum.
   - 6:00 a.m., she hangs it back, perfectly straight.
   - "Martha has done this every night for thirty-one years."
4. **Main conflict:**
   - Leon, 19, is the new night guard and desperate to keep his first job. He notices the frame is crooked by a millimetre each morning.
   - The museum has just announced it is selling the painting, *Woman in Yellow, 1962*, to an anonymous private buyer on Friday.
   - If Leon reports her, she loses her job. If he doesn't and the painting is damaged, he loses his.
5. **Escalation:**
   - The restorer finds fresh fingerprints in the paint, and the director orders an investigation.
   - Leon follows Martha through the rain to a tiny flat. Through the window, an old blind man runs his fingertips over the brushstrokes.
   - The painting is of a young woman in yellow: Martha at twenty. The old man is the painter, who sold it when they were broke. He has lost his sight and is losing his memory, and touching the paint is how he still "sees" her.
   - On the last night before the sale, Leon catches her at the door, and has to decide.
6. **Ending/payoff:**
   - Leon lets her go, and carries the painting for her.
   - In the flat he notices a savings book, which the viewer saw on the kitchen table earlier.
   - Friday: the sale truck pulls up, not at a collector's house but at Martha's flat. She was the anonymous buyer, saving her cleaner's wages for 31 years, and borrowing it every night until she could.
   - Final image: the painting on her wall, the old man's hand on it. Martha says, "You don't have to wait for me to bring her home any more."
7. **Why viewers care:**
   - A small, decent person doing something "wrong" for love, and a young guard whose choice we feel.
   - The payoff turns a theft into devotion, with no villain needed.
8. **Visual opportunities:**
   - A night museum lit by torch beams, and a painting under a coat in the rain.
   - A dust outline on the wall.
   - A UV light revealing 31 years of fingerprints.
   - The blind man's fingers on the paint, mirrored against young Martha in the portrait.
   - The truck pulling up at a council flat.
   - A clear one-location structure, plus the flat.

**Score against One Too Many:** opening 5, premise 5, visual clarity 4, escalation 4, care 5, ending 5 = **28/30**.
**Risk:** it's gentle, with no danger. The tension comes from the guard's choice and the Friday deadline, so the pacing must stay tight.

## 2. The One Rewind
1. **Title:** In This World, Everyone Gets One Rewind
2. **Concept:** Everyone is born with a single 10-second rewind, shown as a gold dot on the wrist that turns grey when used. A girl who has saved hers all her life discovers why her "careless" brother's dot is grey.
3. **Opening hook:** "My brother wasted his rewind at nine years old. On a penalty kick. He missed it again." Rewind, the same miss, and the gold dot fades to grey.
4. **Main conflict:**
   - Maya, 13, has never used her rewind and is proud of it.
   - Her brother Kofi, 16, is the family joke, and she's the hardest on him.
5. **Escalation:**
   - Maya refuses to use her rewind through bigger and bigger temptations: a failed test, a phone dropped in a canal, a humiliating moment in front of the whole school.
   - Winter: Kofi goes out onto the frozen canal after a dog, and the ice cracks under him.
   - Maya uses her rewind. Time runs backward, she grabs his hood, and her dot turns grey.
6. **Ending/payoff:**
   - Afterwards, Mum shows Maya an old video: four-year-old Maya running into the road, then a jump cut. Kofi is suddenly holding her on the pavement, out of breath, and his dot has just turned grey.
   - He never missed that penalty twice. He let everyone laugh at him for seven years so she'd never feel she owed him.
   - Final image: two grey dots, wrists side by side.
7. **Why viewers care:**
   - A sibling relationship everyone recognises.
   - The reveal makes viewers re-watch the opening: the joke was a cover story.
8. **Visual opportunities:**
   - Rewinds shown as everything moving backward (the ball, splashes, falling phones, ice re-sealing).
   - Gold and grey wrist dots as the visible rule, with the same "count" readability as One Too Many's number tags.
   - A frozen canal and cracking ice.
   - A home-video insert.

**Score:** opening 5, premise 5, visual clarity 4, escalation 4, care 4, ending 5 = **27/30**.
**Risks:**
- The world-rule format is close to *Every Lie* and *100 Words*, though the emotional reversal is new.
- The backward-motion shots need testing, though they're likely a cheap reverse of rendered frames.

## 3. The Dog Who Didn't Get the Statue (true story, 1925)
1. **Title:** The Dog Who Didn't Get the Statue
2. **Concept:** In 1925, sled-dog teams raced about 1,000 km through a blizzard to bring medicine to a town where children were dying. The dog who ran the longest and most dangerous part was forgotten.
3. **Opening hook:** "Every dog in this race was a hero. Only one got a statue, and it was the wrong one." A statue in a city park, then a cut to an Alaskan blizzard in 1925.
4. **Main conflict:**
   - Nome, Alaska, January 1925: a diphtheria outbreak, and the only serum is about 1,000 km away.
   - Planes can't fly in the cold, so a relay of dog teams has to carry it.
   - Leonhard Seppala's lead dog Togo is 12 years old, and everyone says he's too old.
5. **Escalation:**
   - −50°C and whiteouts.
   - To save a day, Seppala and Togo cross the frozen sea of Norton Sound in the dark.
   - The ice breaks away with the team on it. Togo leaps the gap and the line snaps; Togo catches the line in his mouth and pulls the ice floe to shore.
   - They run the longest leg of the relay, then hand the serum on.
6. **Ending/payoff:**
   - The serum reaches Nome in five and a half days, and the town is saved.
   - The dog who ran the last, shortest leg into Nome becomes famous and gets the statue. Togo goes home almost unknown.
   - Final line, over Togo resting: "Seppala said it simply. He never had a better dog."
7. **Why viewers care:** dogs, children, an underdog (an old dog nobody believed in), and a real injustice that makes the ending land.
8. **Visual opportunities:**
   - Sled teams in a blizzard.
   - A map line racing across Alaska, a relay baton.
   - Cracking sea ice, and the floe drifting away.
   - The statue reveal bookending the film.

**Score:** opening 4, premise 5, visual clarity 5, escalation 5, care 4, ending 4 = **27/30**.
**Risks:**
- It's well known (there's a 2019 film), so it's less novel.
- Distances and the quote need fact-checking before scripting.
- It needs new dog and sled rigs.

## 4. The Night the Moon Was Late
1. **Title:** The Night the Moon Was Late
2. **Concept:** In a small fishing town, the moon doesn't rise one night, because the old woman who secretly hauled it out of the sea every evening has died. Only the 12-year-old girl next door knows.
3. **Opening hook:** "On Tuesday, the moon didn't rise. And the tide went out, and kept going."
4. **Main conflict:**
   - Ida, 12, always waved to old Mrs Penhallow, who walked down to the beach every evening with a coil of rope. The town thought she was mad.
   - Mrs Penhallow died that morning.
   - Ida knows what the rope was for, and nobody believes a child.
5. **Escalation:**
   - The harbour drains, boats tip over in the mud and fish flap on the sand.
   - Ida pulls alone, and the rope lifts her off her feet.
   - She convinces her friends, then the fishermen. The moon rises halfway and starts slipping.
   - Finally the whole town, the mayor included, is on the rope.
6. **Ending/payoff:**
   - The moon rises and the sea rushes back.
   - Mrs Penhallow's rota is on the harbour wall: one name, every night for sixty years.
   - Final image: a new rota beside it, full of names, with Ida's first.
7. **Why viewers care:**
   - A lonely person nobody valued turns out to have been holding everything up.
   - A child who was right.
   - Must fix: plant Mrs Penhallow warmly in the opening seconds, or we repeat The Other Jamie's mistake.
8. **Visual opportunities:**
   - A giant moon on a rope rising from the sea, and a harbour draining.
   - A tug-of-war of the whole town.
   - Big wide shots and a strong colour shift from moonless dark to moonlight.

**Score:** opening 5, premise 4, visual clarity 5, escalation 4, care 3, ending 4 = **25/30**.
**Risk:** its "fantasy town with a hidden burden" shape is close to *The Whale City*.

## 5. Miss Unsinkable (true story)
1. **Title:** Miss Unsinkable
2. **Concept:** Violet Jessop, a ship's stewardess, was on board for three of the most famous shipping disasters of her time, and survived them all.
3. **Opening hook:** "Violet Jessop survived the Titanic. It wasn't her first disaster at sea, and it wasn't her last."
4. **Main conflict:** a young woman supporting her family by working at sea keeps going back, each time onto a bigger, "safer" ship.
5. **Escalation:**
   - 1911, the *Olympic*: it collides with a warship, but she gets off unhurt.
   - 1912, the *Titanic*: she's ordered into a lifeboat, and an officer drops a baby into her arms.
   - 1916, the *Britannic*, a hospital ship: it hits a mine, and her lifeboat is dragged toward the turning propellers. She jumps, and is pulled under.
6. **Ending/payoff:**
   - She went back to sea anyway, for decades.
   - Years later she wrote that a woman phoned her late one night and asked: "Did you hold a baby on the night the Titanic sank?" "Yes." "I was that baby." Then the woman laughed and hung up.
   - Final image: Violet by the phone.
7. **Why viewers care:** quiet courage, and repeated escalation with a "no way" factor. The ending is intimate and eerie, without anything supernatural.
8. **Visual opportunities:**
   - Three ships, each bigger: a collision, the iceberg night, the propellers.
   - A baby handed down into a lifeboat.
   - A 1950s phone ringing in the dark.

**Score:** opening 5, premise 5, visual clarity 4, escalation 4, care 4, ending 4 = **26/30**.
**Risks:**
- The phone call is her own account and can't be verified, so it must be framed as "she wrote".
- Three disasters can feel like a list unless each one escalates what it costs her.
