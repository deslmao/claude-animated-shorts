# Independent evaluation: The Keeper's Rule
packet: cf2c0e13

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 5, 7, 9, 10
- evidence: Beat 9's voice says "Ellen, love. It's Dad." and the picture note says it is "using Gran's name", but no line or picture before it ever names Gran as Ellen. The deciding clue, "its light is always green", is only a margin note in a beat-5 insert, while the narrator reads other lines over it. The story never says what lighting the lamp does on a ghost night: does it warn boats off the rocks (good) or draw them in (bad)? Beat 10's "the wall phone again" doesn't say whether the coastguard rang back or you called again.
- problem: The twist in beat 9 lands as "some old man knows a name" rather than "Gran's dead father is calling for her", because the name was never set up. Most viewers will miss the green/red rule, which is the story's main clue, because it is small text in a fast insert. And because the danger of lighting the lamp is never stated, viewers can't understand why keeping it dark would ever be right. Ordinary viewers believe a lit lighthouse saves boats.
- fix: Show "Ellen" before beat 9: Gran signs off on the call ("Promise me, love, it's Ellen's rule now"), or the photo caption reads "Ellen & Dad, the WREN". Have the narrator read the margin note aloud in beat 5 ("And in the margin, in Gran's hand: its light is always green."). Add one line or log entry that says what happened the time someone did light the lamp on a ghost night.
### viewer participation
- result: weak
- beats: 5, 6, 8, 10, 11
- evidence: The viewer is invited to tell the ghost from the real boat: the green light (the logbook's ghost) against the red flares and a coastguard you phoned yourself. The final question is "Do you light the lamp for the boat? Or keep your promise and keep it dark?"
- problem: There is a real theory to form, but the choice itself weighs very little. Option B is supported only by an unexplained promise. Option A is supported by real lives, which the coastguard confirms and the flares prove, and by everything viewers believe a lighthouse is for. Most viewers pick A by reflex, without using the clues, so the puzzle never becomes a dilemma.
- fix: Give B a concrete danger that the viewer sees. For example, an old log entry: "1979. Bell rang. Lit the lamp for the green light. Wren hit the rocks." This makes the light itself the lure on ghost nights. Then, to choose A, the viewer has to trust the red-versus-green evidence, not just their instinct.
### cause and effect
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8
- evidence: Each step follows from the last. The bell rings, so the radio call comes. The radio call sends you to the logbook. The logbook ("No boat was ever reported missing") makes you phone the coastguard. The coastguard confirms a real boat, the Wren names itself, and the lights and flares appear.
- problem: The central complication is a coincidence. According to the logbook, the ghost has never come on a night when a real boat was in trouble, yet tonight it does, and nothing links the two. Nor is there any reason why Gran's dead father wants the lamp lit, so his plea in beat 9 has no cause the viewer can read.
- fix: Make the overlap causal. Either the ghost comes because a real boat is in danger and needs a warning, or the ghost's light is what drew the real boat toward the rocks. Plant the link once, in the logbook or in Gran's words, so beat 9's plea has a motive the viewer can judge.
### emotional investment
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 9, 10
- evidence: Gran is in hospital, gripping the screen and insisting. In beat 2 there is the photo of young Gran with her father, "lost at sea, 1979". In beat 9 the dead father pleads in her name. In beat 10, Gran's tablet lies dark.
- problem: Gran's side, and the dead father's pull on her, are moving. But the protagonist's own stake is vague. Breaking the promise costs nothing we can see beyond letting Gran down. The dark tablet hints at something, perhaps that Gran has died, but it isn't resolved, so it reads as a loose end rather than a stake. The feelings belong to Gran's history, not to "you".
- fix: State what you stand to lose if you break the rule: Gran's line "If you light it, you lose more than the boat", or the log entry suggested above. Either make the dark tablet mean something clear (a dropped call: you try to ring Gran and can't get through), or cut it.
### recontextualisation
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 5, 7, 9
- evidence: In beat 7, "This is the Wren" turns the beat-2 photo into the caller's identity, and turns Gran's rule into decades of her refusing her own father. Beat 9's "Ellen, love. It's Dad" makes the rule personal. Beat 8's green light turns the margin note from beat 5 into a working rule.
- problem: None structurally: the reveal grows out of what we saw and contradicts nothing. It does depend on the clarity fixes, though (the name Ellen, the spoken green note), to land with full force.
- fix: None required beyond the clarity fixes.
### setup and payoff
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10
- evidence: The photo of the WREN pays off in beat 7. The green margin note pays off in beat 8. The tablet set down in beat 1 comes back dark in beat 10. "Ellen" in beat 9 has no setup.
- problem: The payoffs are uneven. WREN is over-planted: an insert on the bow name plus the caption "lost at sea, 1979", seconds before a ghost bell, so beat 7 confirms what viewers have already guessed. The green note is planted too faintly. Ellen isn't planted at all, and the dark tablet is a payoff with no clear meaning.
- fix: Remove "lost at sea, 1979" from the photo, show only the boat and the name, and let the date surface in the logbook. Plant "Ellen" (see clarity) and voice the green note. Make the dark tablet mean a dropped call (Gran is out of reach), or drop it.
### visual communication
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10
- evidence: Muted, these read well: the bell swinging with nobody near it, the head turning from the radio to the photo, green light against red flares, backing into the lamp, the hand trembling over the switch. Muted, these are lost: the rule (dialogue only, beat 1), "No boat was ever reported missing" (narration over pages of handwriting), the coastguard's confirmation (phone audio; the picture shows only "you go pale"), and the margin note (handwriting in an insert).
- problem: The two facts the dilemma rests on are an unspoken handwritten note and an audio-only confirmation. On a phone screen, three handwritings and a margin note in a moving insert can't be read in time.
- fix: Hold the beat-5 insert on a single line, with the margin note circled or underlined and the narration reading it, for at least 1.5 s. In beat 6, show something that confirms a real boat, such as the coastguard's words on a notepad you scribble or a harbour board, or keep the audio and accept it. Make sure the green and the red in beat 8 are unmistakably different hues.
### originality
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11
- evidence: The story combines a fog-bell ghost ship, an inherited "rules" warning, a dead relative's voice, and a real boat in danger that has to be told apart from the ghost by its light colour, and it ends on the viewer's choice.
- problem: The ingredients are familiar: John Carpenter's The Fog, phantom-ship legends, and the "if X happens, never do Y" rules-horror format. But the engine, a signal-discrimination puzzle with a family stake, is its own.
- fix: None required.
### accuracy
- result: n/a fiction
- beats: 1-11
- evidence: The packet says factual: no (fiction).
- problem: None as a factual claim. (There's a plausibility note under plot hole: real boats show green sidelights too, and a lighthouse is normally lit every night.)
- fix: None.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the story rests on several obstacles: the dark lamp, Gran being unreachable, and the coastguard's knowledge.
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 6, 8, 10
- finding: (1) A lighthouse is lit every night, and above all in fog, yet the lamp is dark at the start and has to be switched on. The story never explains why it is off before the bell rings. (2) Gran's tablet is right there (beats 1 and 10), so why not call her and ask? The dark tablet hints she can't be reached, but it isn't shown. (3) In beat 10 the coastguard says "they're nearly on the rocks", but with a lifeboat 20 minutes away and thick fog, how would they know? If this hints that the second call is also the ghost, it is far too buried to count as a clue. If it isn't a hint, it is a hole. (4) Real fishing boats carry a green starboard light, so a sea-aware viewer will find the green-equals-ghost rule shaky. Fixes: make the rule "when the bell rings by itself, put the lamp out", so you switch it off in beat 3 and the choice is whether to switch it back on. Show a failed call to Gran. Either let the beat-10 coastguard cite the flares ("we've seen their flares off your point") or plant clearly that the phone can be faked.
### consistency
- applies: yes, the logbook, the photo and the coastguard all make claims that have to agree.
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 5, 6, 7, 10
- finding: Beat 5 says "No boat was ever reported missing", yet beat 2's photo says the WREN was "lost at sea, 1979", and the logbook covers "decades". If the entries begin before 1979, the two contradict. If the Wren was lost on a kept-dark night, that is the story's real secret, and it is neither confirmed nor denied, so the viewer is left unsure. The radio caller in beat 4 ("We've lost our engine!") is never resolved as the ghost or the real boat. The phone in beat 10 is "again", with no sign of who called whom. Fix: date the first log entry after 1979, or make the 1979 entry the explicit exception. Have the beat-7 voice repeat beat 4's words, so we know they're the same caller.
### giveaway
- applies: yes, the identity of the caller is the story's reveal.
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 3, 7
- finding: The beat-2 insert shows the boat's name, WREN, plus "lost at sea, 1979" just before a ghost bell rings, so the beat-7 reveal ("This is the Wren") is announced rather than planted. The solution to the dilemma, green means ghost and red means real, isn't given away. If anything it is under-planted. Fix: show only the boat and its name in the photo, and move the "lost" information later.
### dilemma balance
- applies: yes, the viewer chooses between A, LIGHT THE LAMP, and B, KEEP IT DARK.
- result: fail
- beats: 1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10
- finding: The evidence for A: a coastguard you dialled yourself confirms a real fishing boat missing tonight (beat 6). Real red distress flares appear, unlike the logbook's green ghost light (beat 8). The lifeboat is 20 minutes away (beat 6). "They need that light, now" (beat 10). And viewers already believe a lighthouse's light saves boats. The evidence for B: Gran's promise (beat 1), decades of log entries (beat 5), and the fact that a dead man is asking for the light, which is suspicious but never explained (beat 9). B has no shown consequence: nothing tells the viewer that lighting the lamp has ever hurt anyone. A wins clearly, on instinct alone. Fix: give B a concrete, seen cost (a log entry where lighting the lamp led to a wreck, or the light drawing boats in on ghost nights), so that choosing A takes trust in the red-versus-green evidence.
### redundancy
- applies: yes, several narrator lines sit over shots that show the same thing.
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 6, 8, 10
- finding: Beat 8's "Through the fog, a green light. Then a red flare. Then another." repeats exactly what the shot shows. Beat 10's "The switch is under your hand." describes the insert. Beat 3's "Then the fog rolls in" repeats the picture, though "On its own" earns its place by echoing the rule. Beat 6's "So you phone the coastguard" is mostly description, but the "So" adds the motive, which is acceptable. Fix: spend beat 8's words on the meaning ("Green. Like the logbook said. But flares are red, and ghosts don't fire flares.", or something subtler) and cut beat 10's line, or replace it with a stake.
### originality
- applies: yes, a ghost-ship-in-fog lighthouse story has strong precedents.
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 3, 6, 8, 11
- finding: The closest well-known story is John Carpenter's The Fog (1980): a coastal town, a fog bank, a lighthouse, a bell, the ghosts of drowned sailors on a lost ship. Three structural differences: (1) the ghost pleads for help instead of taking revenge; (2) a real boat in danger appears at the same time, which turns the story into a puzzle of telling the signals apart; (3) the stake is inherited and personal (a hospitalised gran, her father's voice, her rule), and it ends on the viewer's choice. More than the nouns differ.
### emotional cost
- applies: yes, there is a protagonist making a choice.
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 6, 9, 10
- finding: If you keep it dark, the real crew may drown, and that is clearly shown (beats 6, 8 and 10). If you light the lamp, you break a promise to a sick gran, with no consequence ever shown. The protagonist's personal loss is never set out before the climax. The emotional weight sits on Gran and her father, not on "you". Fix: as above, show the cost of lighting the lamp, and make Gran's distance hurt (a failed call, a nurse's voice).
### cold viewer
- applies: yes, always.
- result: weak
- beats: 1-11
- finding: The cold viewer's summary: "I'm minding my gran's lighthouse. On foggy nights a ghost boat, her dead dad's boat the Wren, calls for light, and the family always ignores it. Tonight a real boat is in trouble too, the coastguard confirms it, I see real flares, and I have to choose whether to light the lamp." What they would get wrong or miss: they wouldn't know who "Ellen" is. They would likely miss the green-light rule. They might think the beat-4 caller is the real boat, or that the real missing boat is the Wren. And they wouldn't know why lighting a lighthouse could ever be wrong, so they'd answer A without hesitating.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes. The hook is strong: a sick gran's rule, a bell ringing on its own, a distress call, the coastguard's confirmation, and a dead father's voice keep tightening through the full 11 beats. Viewers would stay to the end, but many would feel the closing choice was a foregone A, and the "Ellen" moment would fall flat.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Give B a concrete, seen cost: one log entry or Gran line showing that lighting the lamp on a ghost night caused harm (for example, it drew the Wren, or another boat, onto the rocks), so choosing A takes trust in the red-versus-green evidence rather than instinct. 2. Make the deciding clues land: have the narrator read the margin note "its light is always green" in beat 5, plant "Ellen" before beat 9, take "lost at sea, 1979" off the beat-2 photo, and replace beat 8's descriptive narration with meaning. 3. Close the logic holes: explain why the lamp is dark (the rule is to put it out when the bell rings, so the choice is to switch it back on), show that Gran can't be reached, give the beat-10 coastguard a reason to know where the boat is (they've seen its flares), and tie the real boat's presence to the ghost night causally rather than by coincidence.
