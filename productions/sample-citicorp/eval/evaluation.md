# Independent evaluation: The Skyscraper Fixed in Secret
packet: d2f8d17f

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 4, 6, 7, 11
- evidence: The building and its owner are never named: beat 7 says "Telling the bank could finish his career" but no bank has been set up (Citicorp is never said). Beat 2 plants "a concrete block on top of the model" with no function; beat 6 then introduces both the 1-in-16 odds and "the block that steadied the tower would stop" in one beat, so the viewer meets the damper's purpose and its failure at the same moment. Beat 4 says the joints "had been bolted instead of welded" "to save money" but never says why that matters, and beat 5 shows the bolted joints glowing red without a mechanism. Beat 11 says "the winds on the flat sides were the bigger load" without saying that a tower built for those winds was safe.
- problem: A cold viewer can't say which building or which bank. They end up with "cheap bolts are weak", which is the wrong mechanism. At beat 6 they have two new things to process (the odds and the block), and at beat 11 "bigger load" can be read as "the building was in more danger than anyone thought".
- fix: Name it once in beat 2 ("Citicorp Center", "the bank's new tower"). At beat 2 or 3 give the block one clause ("a 400-ton block on the roof to calm the sway"). Replace the beat 4 cause with the real one: bolts were fine, but there were too few, sized with no safety margin and never checked for corner winds. In beat 11 say what "bigger load" means: a tower safe in face winds was safe in corner winds.

### viewer participation
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 5, 6, 7, 11
- evidence: The fan and model let the viewer guess the corner-wind problem (beat 3 face → beat 5 corner), and the sixteen-slot wheel (beat 6) is a good piece of evidence the viewer can count. But the one real dilemma is resolved in the same beat it's posed: "Telling the bank could finish his career. / He told them anyway." (beat 7). The beat 11 reversal invites debate, but the next line closes it ("He did what he could with what he knew").
- problem: The viewer never gets a second to ask "what would I do?" Silence is never shown as a real option (nobody else knows, 1 in 16 a year, career and money on one side), so the confession costs the viewer nothing.
- fix: In beat 7, give silence one concrete line before he speaks: what he'd keep by staying quiet and what he'd be betting. Hold the silent phone shot. In beat 11, end on the open, defensible question (was the fix needed?) rather than a verdict.

### cause and effect
- result: weak
- beats: 4, 5, 9, 10
- evidence: Most links are causal: church → mid-side legs → V braces → bolts + corner wind → odds → confession → night welding. But the climax resolves by luck: "Then, with the work half done, Hurricane Ella headed for New York. / It turned out to sea." The secrecy also resolves by luck: "The city's newspapers were on strike that summer." Beat 4's bolt news changes nothing on screen until beat 5 ("his pen freezes" is drama added on top; see accuracy).
- problem: Both of the protagonist's crises (the storm and exposure) are settled by outside chance rather than by anything he did. The real, causal answer, that he had the most critical joints welded first, so the tower could by then survive a 200-year storm, is left out.
- fix: In beat 8 or 9, show the prioritisation (he ranks the joints and the worst one is plated first), so that when Ella comes the tower is already much safer because of his choice, and the turn out to sea is a relief rather than the rescue. Tie the strike to a concrete cause and effect (a Times reporter was chasing him the day the strike began) or cut it.

### emotional investment
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 6, 7
- evidence: The office workers from beat 1 come back in beat 6 at their desks, which is good: lives at risk are shown. But LeMessurier is a name and a drafting table (beat 2) until beat 7, where his stake arrives in one stated line: "Telling the bank could finish his career." Nothing shows his pride in the building or what he'd lose (his name, his firm, the praise), and his choice is made within the same beat.
- problem: We care about the tower's occupants a little, but not about him before the decision. The confession is stated, not felt.
- fix: Plant his pride early: one shot or line in beat 2 or 3 showing that this is his signature work (he slides the church under the overhang with obvious delight, and the line could note the acclaim). In beat 7, show what's at stake visually (his firm's name on the door, the lawyers) before he pushes the papers forward.

### visual communication

### originality
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 7, 11
- evidence: It uses the action-first engine (the strange act, then the reason) of *The Ancient Jar*, but none of its "Don't copy" items. The arc is an engineer finding his own flaw and confessing (beat 7), not ridicule then vindication, and the ending re-examines the premise (beat 11).
- problem: The Citicorp crisis is one of the most retold engineering stories (ethics courses, podcasts, many YouTube explainers), so "seen this" comments are likely. The beat 11 NIST coda is what sets this version apart, and that's exactly where the accuracy problems are.
- fix: (none for originality; the fix to beat 11 under accuracy also protects the differentiator)

### accuracy
- result: fail
- beats: 1, 4, 6, 7, 9, 11
- evidence: Checked against the three cited sources, read in full.
  - (a) Beat 11: "But nobody could have known that in 1978." NIST [N] says the opposite: "their measurements of base moments in the 1970s wind tunnel tests should have led them to the same conclusion that corner winds do not govern the design, but face winds do." The research table quotes N selectively.
  - (b) Beat 11: "engineers re-ran his numbers with modern tools". NIST used a new method (Database-Assisted Design) on wind-tunnel data from "a skyscraper of similar proportions", because the original tests "remain confidential". It's a new analysis, not a re-run of his numbers. NIST's finding is also conditional ("if the building had been safely designed for head-on winds") and calls for re-examination rather than settling the question.
  - (c) Beat 4: "To save money, the joints… had been bolted instead of welded", followed by the corner-wind failure, implies bolts were the defect. M says bolted joints "are more practical and equally safe" and the change "was technically sound". The defect was too few bolts: the team had classed the braces as trusses, which skipped the column safety factor, and never checked quartering winds (M; W: "truss safety factor… instead of the column safety factor"). The script omits this.
  - (d) Beat 4's picture ("his pen freezes mid-note") contradicts M: "This news gave LeMessurier no cause for concern in the days immediately following the meeting."
  - (e) Beat 6 says one in sixteen, then "And if the power failed, the block… would stop", implying the damper was running in the 1-in-16 case. M and W: 1 in 16 was the case without the damper (no power); 1 in 55 with it running.
  - (f) Beat 7: "Nobody else had put it together." This is LeMessurier's own claim (M: "I had information that nobody else in the world had") stated as fact. M also says Davenport knew, and W says the student Diane Hartley had calculated quartering stresses above the firm's figures.
  - (g) Beat 9: "with the work half done" omits that by then "the most critical joints had already been fixed" and the tower "could now withstand a two-hundred-year storm" (M; W agrees). The clenched-fist peril overstates the risk.
  - (h) Beat 1's picture (a welder pulls the plywood shut as the lift opens at 7 a.m.) contradicts M: welding ran 8 p.m. to 4 a.m., then crews cleaned up "before the first secretaries arrived". No welders hid in the boxes in the daytime.
  - Correct: the church and mid-side columns, V braces, May bolts and June student (the neutral wording is fine; W now also names Lee DeCarolis, 2022, as the caller), 40% in four of eight chevrons, generators and forecasters, unpublicised evacuation plans, Ella on 1 Sept turning out to sea, the strike and October completion, 1995.
- problem: The story's quotable moral (beat 11) is contradicted by the source it cites for that beat. That's the "make the quotable ending defensible" risk in benchmarks §4.10, and an engineer in the comments will quote NIST back. The bolts-to-save-money framing repeats a popular myth that M explicitly corrects. Several dramatisations (the frozen pen, welders hiding by day, peak danger at Ella) change what happened.
- fix:
  - Beat 11: replace "nobody could have known" with what NIST says, e.g. "NIST says the clue was already in the 1970s wind-tunnel data. In 1978 he had a risk, not an answer, and he reported it." Change "re-ran his numbers" to "re-checked with a new method".
  - Beat 4: say "bolts were fine; too few were used, and nobody checked corner winds".
  - Beat 6: reorder to "1 in 16 if the power failed and the block stopped".
  - Beat 7: attribute the line ("He believed only he knew") or cut it.
  - Beat 9: add "the worst joints were already done".
  - Beat 4 picture: change the pen freeze to an unconcerned note.
  - Beat 1 picture: show the box empty at 7 a.m. and the line as "By morning, only plywood boxes were left."

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, a factual engineering story with an obstacle (an unsafe tower) and a fix
- result: weak
- beats: 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11
- finding: Logic gaps a cold viewer hits: why are bolts dangerous if they're a normal way to build (beat 4)? Why didn't anyone just evacuate or close the building (beat 6 to 8: the story never says why occupation continued, which was the damper plus generators)? Why does the 1-in-16 storm suddenly not matter when Ella arrives (beat 9)? The top-comment probe would pick at: (1) "Bolted joints aren't weaker than welds; the real error was the truss safety factor" (true; see accuracy); (2) "NIST literally says they should have known" against beat 11; (3) "So the repairs were pointless?" (beat 11 invites it without an answer); (4) "Say the name: Citicorp Center / give Diane Hartley credit"; (5) "They hid the evacuation plan from the neighbourhood and told the press there was 'no danger'. That's not heroic." The heroic ending ("He did what he could") ignores that documented criticism.

### consistency
- applies: yes, the story references earlier tests, months, objects and the model across 11 beats
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 5, 6, 7, 11
- finding: Beats 3 and 11 conflict visually: the same fan on the same flat face of the same model is harmless in 1978 and "redder than the corner" in the modern lab, with no visible change of method. Beat 6's odds and power-failure order doesn't match beat 2's unexplained block and the research. "The bank" (beat 7) refers to nothing established. The timeline holds: "That May" (beat 4) → "in June" (beat 5) → nightly welding (beat 8) → Ella (beat 9) → completion before the papers return (beat 10). Beat 1's "all night" matches beat 8's "every night".

### giveaway
- applies: yes, the packet flags beat 4 against beat 8
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 4, 8
- finding: Beat 4 names the flaw (bolted joints), not the solution. The solution's image (welders in plywood boxes) is deliberately shown first in beat 1 as the action-first hook, and its meaning (plates over the bolts) is withheld until beat 8. That's planting, not announcing.

### dilemma balance
- applies: no, there's no on-screen choice and no competing explanations for the viewer to weigh; the only dilemma (beat 7, confess or stay silent) is the protagonist's, and he resolves it himself
- result: pass
- beats: 7
- finding: n/a. Note under participation: silence is never given any weight, so even the internal dilemma is one-sided.

### redundancy
- applies: yes, the narration runs over illustrative shots throughout
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 5, 8, 10
- finding: Mild only. "It turned out to sea" (beat 10) over the spiral bending away, and "He'd tested it with wind hitting the flat sides" (beat 3) over the fan at the flat face, largely describe the shot, but both are short and anchor the facts for viewers who aren't watching closely. "So every night, welders sealed thick steel plates over the bolted joints" (beat 8) adds the meaning of the beat 1 image. Nothing worth cutting for redundancy alone.

### originality
- applies: yes
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 7, 9, 11
- finding: The closest benchmark is *The Ancient Jar That Warned an Empire* (action-first: an engineer, an object, a mechanism explained after the act). Structural differences: (1) there's no ridicule escalation; the protagonist discovers and confesses his own flaw (beat 7); (2) the disaster is averted and hidden (beats 8 to 10), rather than happening and then being explained; (3) the ending re-examines the premise (beat 11) instead of vindicating the hero. None of the Jar's "Don't copy" items (the jar, dragons, exile) is reused, and more than the nouns differ. The outside risk is that the Citicorp crisis itself is widely retold.

### emotional cost
- applies: yes, a protagonist making a costly choice
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 6, 7
- finding: If he fails, the tower may fall on the workers (shown: beat 1 and the beat 6 cut back to them), and if he confesses he may lose his career (beat 7: "could finish his career", stated once, at the moment of choice). Lives are shown before the climax; his personal cost isn't shown at all, only spoken in the decision beat. The research's stronger stakes (bankruptcy, disgrace, and the fact that only he knew) are softened or unused.

### cold viewer
- applies: yes
- result: weak
- beats: 1 to 11
- finding: Summary as a first-time viewer would give it: "In 1978 an engineer found that his New York skyscraper could blow over in a corner wind, because builders had cheaply bolted its braces. He confessed, and it was secretly welded at night while a hurricane nearly hit and a newspaper strike kept it quiet until 1995. Decades later, scientists found the corner winds weren't the real danger, but nobody could have known." Misunderstandings:
  - bolts are inherently weaker and cost-cutting caused the crisis (false);
  - the strike kept it secret for 17 years (the packet's own research says this isn't claimed, but the order of beat 10's lines implies it);
  - the tower was at 1-in-16 risk when Ella came (it was already reinforced to a 200-year storm);
  - "bigger load on the flat sides" means the building was secretly in danger the other way;
  - "nobody could have known" (contradicted by NIST);
  - which building and which bank.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes. The beat 1 hook (welders vanishing into plywood boxes overnight, workers strolling past with coffee) is strong, and beats 5 to 9 escalate well (red joints, the sixteen-slot wheel, the block stopping, the confession, the hurricane). The likely drop is at beats 2 to 4: three exposition beats (church, legs, braces, testing, a meeting about bolts) before the danger appears. The ending gets debated for the wrong reason: viewers will correct it.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes:
  1. Rewrite beat 11 to match NIST.
     - Drop "nobody could have known in 1978": NIST says the 1970s wind-tunnel data "should have led them" to see that face winds govern.
     - Say "re-checked with a new method", not "re-ran his numbers".
     - Say plainly that a tower safe in face winds was safe in corner winds.
     - Redesign the picture so it isn't the same fan-and-model test giving a different answer (for example a model covered in sensors, with the face result within the design).
     - End on a defensible line ("He had a risk, not an answer, and he reported it").
  2. Fix the mechanism and the odds.
     - Bolts weren't the defect: the team used too few, with no safety margin (braces classed as trusses), and never checked corner winds (say it in one line at beat 4 or 5, and replace the "pen freezes" picture).
     - Reorder beat 6 so that 1 in 16 is clearly the no-power case (1 in 55 with the damper running).
     - Give the roof block its one-clause function when it's planted in beat 2.
     - Name Citicorp Center and the bank once.
  3. Earn the climax and the stakes.
     - Show that he had the worst joints welded first, so that by Ella the tower could survive a 200-year storm; the resolution then comes from his choice, not only from the storm's turn.
     - Give silence one concrete line before "He told them anyway", and attribute "Nobody else had put it together" ("he believed only he knew") or cut it.
     - Fix beat 1's daytime-hiding image (by morning only the boxes are left).
