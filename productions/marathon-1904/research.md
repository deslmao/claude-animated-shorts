# The 1904 Olympic marathon: research

## Summary
The men's marathon at the St. Louis Olympics (30 August 1904) was run in about 90 °F heat on a hilly course of dusty
roads, with cars churning up dust alongside the runners and only one water source, a well at about the
12-mile (halfway) mark. 32 men started and 14 finished. Fred Lorz crossed the line first but had ridden much of
the course in a car. He was disqualified and banned for life (the ban was soon reduced), and won the 1905 Boston
Marathon legitimately. The official winner, Thomas Hicks, was kept going by his handlers with strychnine sulphate
(a legal stimulant then), egg white and brandy. He finished in 3:28:53, the slowest Olympic winning time. Cuba's
Félix Carvajal ran in cut-down street clothes after losing his money gambling on the way, ate rotten apples, lay down
for a nap and still finished fourth. Len Taunyane of South Africa was chased about a mile off course by a dog or dogs
and finished ninth. William Garcia nearly died from the dust (a stomach haemorrhage).

## Claims NOT used (unverified, disputed or too long)
- "Hicks never ran again" / weight lost (8 lb): reported in some retellings; not needed and not cross-checked.
- "Hicks was carried over the line": Wikipedia records allegations (Genty) that officials carried him for much of the
  final stretch, but accounts differ. The script only says he "staggered home".
- Carvajal "stole peaches from officials' car": only in some retellings; not used.
- "First Black Africans at the Olympics": generally credited to Taunyane and Mashiani, but not needed. Avoided to keep
  the line short and unqualified.
- "First recorded drug use in the modern Olympics": Smithsonian says so, but "first" claims are hard to prove.
- Number of nations (sources give 4 or 7) and Albert Corey's nationality (French, listed for the USA): not used.
- "The car ride was 11 vs 10 miles": the script uses eleven, the figure in Olympics.com and Wikipedia.

### Dog vs dogs: decision
- **Singular ("a dog"):** Smithsonian (Karen Abbott, 2012) and Wikipedia's race page.
- **Plural ("a pack of wild dogs"):** Olympics.com and ABC News (2020).
- **Olympedia** (the results historians' database, the strongest source for the race record) does not mention the
  incident at all. I could not reach the 1905 primary account (Lucas) or a 1904 newspaper from this session.
- **Decision: "a dog".** Smithsonian is the strongest *documented* account. It is a researched history piece built on
  the 1905 Lucas report and contemporary newspapers (the St. Louis Post-Dispatch), while the Olympics.com and ABC
  pieces are uncited features. Wikipedia agrees with the singular. "Wild" is left out because it is the most
  embellished part in every version.
- **What would settle it:** the Lucas text (*The Olympic Games 1904*, 1905) or a 1904 newspaper report, both
  unreachable from this session (the network blocks those hosts).
