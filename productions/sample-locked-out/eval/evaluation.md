# Independent evaluation: Locked Out
packet: a3ed03d

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 6, 8, 10
- evidence: Beat 1's opening line ("a man is paying me double to break into his own house") plus the on-call text and the readable FINAL WARNING letter set up who (a night locksmith), where (9 Alder Row, 02:14) and the stake (one more refusal and he's dismissed) in one beat. Beat 3 says the stake out loud ("One more refused job and I'm fired"). Beat 8 explains its own mechanism: KL is "my firm", and the History entry is read for the viewer ("She changed the lock yesterday. And kept the keys from him"), so the R. Marsh / D. Marsh difference never depends on reading initials on a phone. In beat 10, "She called us too" after a visible 999 dial makes clear who "us" is.
- problem: none. Beat 8 is the densest beat (stamp, History, the lie to him, his shadow), but it plays as one discovery in order, not two unexplained things.
- fix: none
### viewer participation
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 14
- evidence: The viewer is shown evidence they can check and that cuts both ways. The licence matches the brass 9 and the MARSH bell label (3). The dog's tail thumps at his voice, and the child's drawing shows DADDY in a green coat while he stands there in a green coat (5). Against that: the strip of light upstairs (1) next to "She won't come down" (4), a bright new cylinder in old paint with filings on the mat (6), and the light going out with the curtain twitching (7). The viewer forms a theory (is she hiding from him?) before the narrator confirms it. The countdown (10, 12, 14, 15, 17) is a number they can track themselves.
- problem: none
- fix: none
### cause and effect
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17
- evidence: Every turn follows from what the viewer has seen. He wants it quiet (2), so he puts the brick down the moment he thinks the quiet way is back on (13). The locksmith's excuse (9) leaves Marsh alone, so he goes to the window (11). A horn from the van would push him through the glass (11), so the locksmith goes back out to stall (12, 13). The stall fails ("You're not even trying", 14), so the locksmith first puts himself between Marsh and the window, and only then makes the noise Marsh fears (15). The noise brings witnesses, and that, not luck, makes Marsh let go (16). The police arrive on a timer the locksmith started himself (10, 17). Phase-1 finding I1 (the climax is a gesture and the timer does the work) is resolved: the climax act now causes Marsh's retreat, and the protagonist's choices fill the gap until the police come.
- problem: none at criterion level. The one convenience, that Marsh called the same firm that fitted the lock, is covered under plot hole.
- fix: none
### emotional investment
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 5, 11, 14, 16, 19
- evidence: His job is on the line from the first frame (the visor letter in 1 and 3), and his professional decency shows in small acts ("I always ring first", 4). The family is unseen but concrete: the child's drawing, the small wellies (5), "her little boy" (10), and a brick weighed at their window (11). Before the climax he takes a physical risk (the grip on his shoulder in 14, being slammed against the wall in 16). The final image pays off the people we've been protecting: mother, son and dog at the lit window (19).
- problem: none. The job stake weakens once the police are involved (see emotional cost), but the family's danger carries the climax.
- fix: none
### recontextualisation
- result: pass
- beats: 4, 7, 9, 18, 19
- evidence: "Everything he's told me is true" (9) recasts every earlier proof (the licence, the bell, the dog, the drawing) as true and still wrong. "She won't come down. Not at this hour" (4) takes on a new meaning: she's hiding. The woman's line, "I watched you from upstairs. When you got your tools out" (18), turns the light going out and the curtain twitch (7) from "someone's awake" into "she was watching and calling the police on me". The last line, "I checked who he was. I never asked who the lock was for" (19), reframes the beat 3 check ("Proof of address. His is perfect"). None of this contradicts anything we saw.
- problem: none
- fix: none
### setup and payoff
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 2, 6, 8, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17, 19

Marsh's finger to his lips aimed at the neighbours' dark windows (2, "he wants it quiet") pays off as the window-by-window cascade (15, 16). The brick has three tracked positions (11, 13 dropped by the path, 14 his eyes go to it) before the block in 15. The countdown starts in 10 and lands at 00:00 in 17. The warning letter (1) returns under REFUSED BY TECHNICIAN. REVIEW REQUIRED (19). Nothing appears at the last second.

- fix: none
### visual communication
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 6, 8, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19
- evidence: Muted, the film still reads: a man hushing at the door, a lock that is visibly new, the locksmith hiding his phone screen, a 999 dial and a watch counting down, a brick at the glass, fake picking (the pick beside the keyway, not in it), a man rising to block, windows lighting one after another, the grip releasing, blue light, and the family at the window. Each beat names its key subject, and the camera is on it.
- problem: minor. Beat 15's logic, "Now I'm between him and that window", depends on geography. A bay window sits on the same wall as the door, and the brick is by the path, so "stepping out onto the path" doesn't obviously block him. Beat 16 then has the locksmith pinned against the porch wall, which looks like the block has collapsed.
- fix: optional polish. In beat 15's wide, stage the locksmith clearly in front of the bay window with the brick at his feet and Marsh boxed in at the door, so muted viewers see the block. Keep that geography when Marsh lunges in 16.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the story turns on why the locksmith doesn't simply open the door, honk, or stay in the van, and on why Marsh doesn't just break in
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 6, 8, 11, 12
- finding: Most of the obvious objections are answered on screen. He wants it quiet, so he doesn't smash in at the start (2). A horn from the van would only make him smash in sooner (11). The locksmith leaves the van against instructions because he's the only thing keeping Marsh at the door (12). Phase-1 finding I2 is largely resolved. (1) The job now arrives as a text to the on-call phone, and History is a check he makes himself after recognising his firm's stamp, so no record ever told him not to open. (2) The phone is angled away (8) and pocketed face-in (12). (3) The horn is weighed out loud (11). (4) "No invoice, no call to the office" replaces "no paperwork". What remains is one convenience the story depends on: of all the locksmiths, Marsh texts the firm that fitted his wife's lock the day before, and nothing explains it. Top-comment probe: "He called the SAME company that changed the lock?" A smaller probe: why didn't she call the police the moment he arrived? Beat 18 answers it (the tools were the trigger).
- fix: add one line or picture that makes the shared firm natural rather than lucky. For example, Marsh in beat 2: "Yours was the only number that answered", or KESTREL LOCKS, 24-HOUR on the van in beat 1's windscreen shot, so the viewer infers the only night locksmith in town.
### consistency
- applies: yes, there are times, a countdown, object positions and a "true" claim to check
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 17, 18
- finding: The text's 02:14 matches the line. The upstairs light is on from beat 1, goes out in 7 and comes back on in 18. The licence is taken (3) and handed back (3). The pick case goes to the van (9) and comes back (13). The brick moves glass (11), path (13), path (14). The watch counts 04:00, 03:59, 03:10, 02:20, 01:40, 01:30, 00:00, with plausible gaps for the action between. The KL stamp is on the cylinder face, where a torch can see it. "She won't come down" and "Everything he's told me is true" agree. On phase-1 finding I3, all six items are resolved: the lie, the sticker on the door's edge, the light, the thumb-turn, the licence and the tool bag. One small residual: "Just stopped working tonight" (6) implies a fault, and the narrator answers "New locks don't just stop working", so a pedant could call it a lie before beat 9's "Everything he's told me is true". It is literally true of his key. Optional tweak: "Key won't go in tonight."
### giveaway
- applies: yes, the climax solution ("make it loud") rests on an early observation
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 11, 15
- finding: "And he wants it quiet" (2) is planted as a character note, not as a solution. Beat 11 then argues against noise (the horn would make it worse), which misleads the viewer. Beat 15 turns it around only once the locksmith has physically put himself between Marsh and the window. Early suspicion of Marsh is intended tension against the proofs, not a giveaway.
### dilemma balance
- applies: no, the story has no on-screen choice and the viewer doesn't pick between explanations at the end; it ends on its last beat
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 5, 8, 9, 10
- finding: The "is he legitimate?" question is deliberately settled at the midpoint (8 to 10). After that the story becomes a four-minute standoff with its own question. The evidence-balance flags are dismissed below for that reason.
### redundancy
- applies: yes, the narration often runs over inserts
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 7, 8, 11, 15
- finding: The flagged lines add meaning. Beat 8 turns "Customer: R. Marsh. Keys issued to R. Marsh only" into who and why ("She… kept the keys from him"). Beat 11 gives the reason the hand stops over the horn, which the shot can't show. The lines closest to pure description are "Someone's awake up there" (7) and "Now I'm between him and that window" (15). Each still adds an inference or a strategy, and each is fine.
### reproduction
- applies: yes, three inspirations are declared and Tower 9 and The Last Stop are the closest
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 8, 10, 15, 19
- finding: Compared with Tower 9 and The Last Stop on all five points. Sequence: different (here the protagonist stands outside with the suspect and is asked to do the opening). Motivations: a tradesman's job and his conscience, against an estranged father who wants in. Central conflict: true proofs that are still wrong, not which voice is lying. Solution: stall, block, wake the street. Meaning of the payoff: the lock belongs to the person it was fitted for. None of the benchmarks' distinctive expression is reused. Hale, the :07 time and the duplicated mum are all absent. The familiar "locksmith used by an abuser" premise is a trope, not a reproduction.
### emotional cost
- applies: yes, a protagonist with a stated stake
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 9, 11, 14, 16, 19
- finding: If he fails, a mother and child are exposed to a man with a brick (5, 10, 11), and the locksmith takes physical harm (14, 16). His job is shown before the climax (1, 3), and refusing a customer with perfect ID at beat 9 is a real risk. On phase-1 finding I4: the firm's record no longer orders a refusal, so the stake survives longer, and the coda puts it back in view (REVIEW REQUIRED under the warning letter, 19). Residual: once the 999 operator says "Do not open her door" (12), refusing costs him nothing at the climax, so the job stake goes quiet from beat 10 to 18.
- fix: optional. Keep the job stake alive in the climax with Marsh's own words in beat 14, for example "And your boss hears you refused me", so the threat reaches back to the visor letter.
### cold viewer
- applies: yes
- result: pass
- beats: all
- finding: A first-time viewer would think: "A night locksmith is offered double cash to let a man into his own house. Every proof checks out, but the new lock was fitted by the locksmith's own firm yesterday for the man's wife, who kept the keys from him. He calls the police, stalls the man, and when the man threatens to smash in, blocks the window and shouts the street awake until the police take him away; the wife and son are safe, and he logs the refusal even though it may cost him his job." Possible misreadings: viewers may wonder why the shout is safe when the horn wasn't (the beat 15 line covers it, and clearer staging would too), and may briefly read Marsh backing away (16) as an escape before the police catch him (17). Neither breaks the story.

## Automated flags

- possible redundancy, beat 11 "A horn, and he's through that glass…": dismissed, it gives the reasoning behind the hand stopping above the horn, which no picture can show
- evidence balance, 3 for A vs 6 for B: dismissed, there's no viewer choice, and the evidence is meant to converge on Marsh being dangerous at the midpoint
- evidence balance, last evidence (beat 9) only supports B: dismissed, settling the identity question at 8 to 10 is the structural turn into the standoff, not a choice decided too early
- motivation not visible in the first 7 beats: dismissed, the want and the pressure are shown and said in beats 1 and 3 (the readable FINAL WARNING letter, "One more refused job and I'm fired")

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes, the first line is a whole situation with a stake and a question. A new fact or proof lands every beat, and the countdown pulls through the second half. The likeliest drop-off is beat 12 (a reflective decision beat), but it's short and the timer is running.
- verdict: ready
- top-fixes: 1. Explain the shared firm in one line or picture (e.g. Marsh: "Yours was the only number that answered", or KESTREL LOCKS, 24-HOUR on the van in beat 1) so "he called the same company?" doesn't become the top comment. 2. Stage beat 15's wide so the block reads muted: the locksmith in front of the bay window, the brick at his feet, Marsh boxed in at the door, with the same geography kept through the lunge in 16. 3. Keep the job stake alive into the climax with a Marsh threat in beat 14 ("And your boss hears you refused me"), and optionally change "Just stopped working tonight" to "Key won't go in tonight" so beat 9's "Everything he's told me is true" holds up.
