# Video 04 script review: The Worst Olympic Marathon Ever

`script.md` is unchanged (the original, as sent). This file holds my assessment and **recommended** changes only;
none of them is applied. Line numbers refer to the 15 lines of `script.md`.

## Assessment

### Opening hook and the first 3 seconds
- Line 1: "The first man to finish the 1904 Olympic marathon had spent eleven miles in a car." (16 words, inside the
  16-word hook limit)
- It is accurate and the twist is strong, but the twist ("in a car") is the **last** phrase. At George's pace
  (with "1904" spoken as "nineteen-oh-four") it lands at about 4.5 s. The first 3 seconds carry only "The first man to
  finish the 1904 Olympic…", which is setup without surprise.
- On screen the first 3 seconds can still sell it (a car rolling across a finish line, the "FINISH" sign, a swoop
  onto the driver), but the words lag the picture.

### Pacing and curiosity
- Strong spine: the setup (heat, one water stop), then the cheat (Lorz), the "real" winner who is no better (Hicks), the
  side stories (Carvajal, Taunyane), the pivot (why it went so wrong), and the payoff (Lorz wins Boston on foot).
- Each section opens a new question ("who really won?", "how was he still standing?"), which keeps curiosity up.
- Lines 10 to 12 are a rapid list (Carvajal, Taunyane, fourteen finishers). That is fine for a Short, but the storyboard must
  give each its own picture (apple macro, dog chase swoop, a board showing "14 / 32"), or it will feel like a list.
- Line 3 ("study dehydration") is the most abstract line. It is accurate and hedged, but slightly dry.

### Are the events engaging and easy to understand?
- Yes. Each runner gets one concrete, visual oddity (car, poison and brandy, rotten apples and a nap, dogs). No
  sports knowledge is needed.
- "Strychnine" is explained in the same breath ("Rat poison, but legal back then"), so viewers are not lost.
- Line 13 ("nobody knew how to run a marathon safely yet") is a slight over-generalisation; see R3.

### Factual accuracy and conflicting sources
Every claim is in `research.md` with at least two sources, except the dehydration motive, which has one strong source
and is hedged with "reportedly". Places where sources conflict and how the script handles them:

| Claim | Conflict | Script's handling |
|---|---|---|
| Lorz's car ride | 11 miles (Olympics.com, Wikipedia) vs about 10 (one Wikipedia mention) | uses the majority figure, "eleven miles" |
| Dogs that chased Taunyane | a pack of wild dogs (Olympics.com) vs one wild dog (Smithsonian) | "dogs" (IOC wording); see R4 |
| Lorz's ban | lifetime ban **commuted to six months** (Wikipedia) vs "lifted" (Olympics.com) | "lifted"; see R5 |
| Water | one official stop / well at about 12 miles; Carvajal drank at a water tower at about 6 miles | "one place to get water": see R2 |
| Dehydration motive | Smithsonian only | "reportedly" |
| Hicks carried over the line | alleged by some, disputed | not used ("staggered home") |

### Is the ending memorable?
- Yes. "And Lorz? His ban was lifted, and next spring he won the Boston Marathon. On foot." It calls back to the hook
  (the car), turns the cheat into a redemption joke, and ends on a two-word button. It is the strongest line in the
  script.

### Estimated runtime
- Script tool estimate: **64.0 s** (197 words).
- Calibration: past finals ran longer than their estimates. Video 03 was estimated at 61.3 s and finished at 64.9 s
  (+3.6 s); Pirate Democracy was estimated at 61.4 s and finished at 62.4 s (+1.0 s).
- **Expected final: about 65–68 s**, at or slightly over the 55–65 s target. I recommend trimming about 8–10 words
  (R6) so the final lands near 62–64 s.

## Recommended changes (not applied: for your decision)

**R1. Hook: land the twist inside 3 seconds.** Two options:
- (a) Minimal: "In 1904, the Olympic marathon's first finisher rode eleven miles in a car." (13 words; "car" lands at
  about 3.5 s)
- (b) Cold-open montage: "A car ride. Rat poison. A nap. This was the 1904 Olympic marathon." (12 words; three
  surprises in the first 3 s, each a 1-second swoop or macro shot). Line 1's car fact is then delivered in the Lorz section
  (lines 4–5 already cover it).
- My preference is (b). It opens on pictures, suits the new swoop and macro camera moves, and makes viewers wait for
  three answers.

**R2. Line 2: "one place to get water" → "one official water stop".** This is more precise, since Carvajal drank from a
water tower along the way. It is also the Smithsonian wording. +1 word.

**R3. Line 13: soften the over-generalisation.** "You see, nobody knew how to run a marathon safely yet." →
"You see, the marathon was still new, and nobody knew how to run one safely." The modern Olympic marathon began
in 1896, eight years earlier; I would add a source for that before using it. +4 words.

**R4. Line 11: "dogs" vs "a dog".** Keep "dogs" (the IOC's own account), or use "a dog" (Smithsonian). Both are
sourced; the research file records the disagreement either way. No change is needed unless you prefer the singular.

**R5. Line 15: "His ban was lifted" → "His ban was cut short".** This is more exact (a lifetime ban reduced to six months)
and the same length. Optional: "lifted" is how Olympics.com puts it.

**R6. Runtime trims (about 8–10 words), if adopting R2 or R3 add words:**
- Line 8: "got egg whites, brandy and strychnine from his team" → "was fed egg whites, brandy and strychnine" (−3)
- Line 2: "St. Louis. Thirty-two runners, ninety-degree heat, dusty roads…" → "Thirty-two runners, ninety-degree
  heat, dusty roads…" (−2; St. Louis can go on the "FINISH" sign or a board)
- Line 13: "No proper water, no drug rules, and dust from cars nearly killed one runner." → "No proper water, no drug
  rules, and car dust nearly killed one runner." (−2)
- Line 12: "Only fourteen finished." could be shown on a board ("14 / 32") instead of spoken (−3)

**R7. Casting (storyboard, not script).** Line 6 gives Milo the line "It was a joke!", so Milo plays Lorz. The
storyboard should keep each historical figure on one character throughout (for example Milo as Lorz, Gus as
Hicks), so viewers can follow who is who.
