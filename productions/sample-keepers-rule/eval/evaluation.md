# Independent evaluation: The Keeper's Rule
packet: f5338a69

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 5, 9
- evidence: Beat 1 has three voices in one beat (narrator, then the nurse's "Ellen, medicine time", then Gran's promise) and the only sign that Gran is Ellen is the nurse line plus a wristband on a tablet screen. Beat 9's payoff, "Ellen, love. It's Dad.", only makes sense once the viewer has put together Ellen = Gran, Gran = the child who wrote "Dad never came back down", and the voice = her dead father. Beat 5's "its light is always green" has no clear antecedent ("its" = the voice? the thing?). Geography: in beat 1 you're beside the lamp switch, in beat 2 you climb up the stairs toward the lamp, and in beat 3 you pull the switch again.
- problem: A cold viewer who misses the nurse line (it's spoken over the call, in the middle of the hook) will think *you* are Ellen. Then "It's Dad" reads as your father, which nothing explains, and the strongest beat in the film turns confusing. The switch moving between beats 1 and 3 makes it unclear whether you're at the top or the bottom of the tower, which matters when the threat "came up the steps".
- fix: Hold the wristband insert long enough to read, and add a second, unmissable "Ellen = Gran" moment (see the plot-hole fix: the ward nurse on the landline in beat 5 says "Ellen's sedated, love"). Put beat 2's climb before the call, so that beat 1 happens at the top beside the switch. Change "its light" to "Its lights are always green" or "The voice's light is always green".

### cause and effect
- result: weak

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, there's a concrete rescue mechanism (the light saves the boat) and several ways to reach help
- result: fail
- beats: 4, 5, 7, 10
- finding: (1) "Our engine's gone": a drifting boat can't avoid rocks by seeing them, so the coastguard's "Light it now!" has no logic. That will be the top comment ("how does a light fix an engine?"). (2) The coastguard has them on radar and can presumably radio them their position and a heading, so why is the lamp the only hope? (3) The wall phone works, but you never call the hospital, after Gran conveniently has "No signal". (4) Lighthouses going dark on every foggy night for decades, with nobody noticing, is a lesser gap but a likely comment. Fixes: make the boat's problem blindness, not propulsion; have the coastguard say "They're not answering us, only you"; and add the ward call on the landline.
### consistency
- applies: yes, the story references times, distances, places and an earlier generation
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 10
- finding: The lamp switch is beside you in beat 1, you climb toward the lamp in beat 2, and you pull the switch in beat 3: the order or the location contradicts itself. In beat 8 the coastguard explains a green light he can't see and that no line has reported to him. The boat drifts from half a mile (beat 7) to 200 m (beat 10) within a few lines, which is fast but tolerable. Gran was a child in 1979, so she's about 55 now: the hospital-bed Gran must be drawn to that age, or the date moved earlier (e.g. 1962). The tablet works in beat 1 and has no signal in beat 5, with no cause shown.
### giveaway
- applies: yes, the logbook plants the green light and the calling voice before they appear
- result: pass
- beats: 5, 8, 9
- finding: The plants create dread rather than solving anything. The green light gets a true, mundane counter-explanation (starboard), and the voice's identity (Gran's dead father, calling her name) isn't announced. There's no single "solution" to give away; it's an open dilemma.
### dilemma balance
- applies: yes, the viewer chooses between switching on and keeping it dark
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11
- finding: For A (switch on): radar confirms a real boat with four people, from a source you called yourself; the green light is plausibly her starboard light; the urgency of 200 m. For B (keep dark): the bell rang with no wind; the logbook shows the same pattern over decades; in 1979 her father lit it and never came back down; a creak on the stairs; the radio, the source of the original distress call, speaks with the dead father's voice. Neither side clearly wins. But the two sides answer different questions (is the boat real? yes; is the thing real? yes), so the cards' "trust" framing is a little off. "Trust a radar screen over a dead man's warning" is also confusing because the dead man's *voice* has just urged lighting it. The real choice is four strangers' lives against your own life and Gran's promise; phrase the cards that way, in parallel and with consequences.
### redundancy
- applies: yes, the narration often narrates the action on screen
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 5, 7, 8
- finding: "At midnight, the fog rolls in" (the shot shows the fog), "You try Gran. No signal." (the tablet shows NO SIGNAL), "So you open her logbook" and "So you call the coastguard" (both shown), and "Through the fog, a light." (shown). "By itself" and "Green." add meaning, so keep them. Trim the "So you…" lines and use the seconds for the ward call or the clarity fixes.
### originality
- applies: yes
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 6, 9, 11
- finding: The closest is Tower 9 (fire lookout); The Last Stop is second (evidence that cuts both ways, parallel final options). Three structural differences: (1) the dilemma puts third-party lives, confirmed independently by radar, against the rule, rather than only self-protection; (2) the key clue (green light) has two valid explanations, instead of a lie exposed by a checkable fact; (3) a generational logbook and a family loss (the 1979 page, the wristband) supply the recontextualisation. No literal "Don't copy" items are reused (no fire tower, Dale, beans or trapdoor), but the "something climbing the stairs of a lone tower while a familiar voice calls" frame echoes Tower 9's stairs and trapdoor. The impersonated-relative voice is adjacent to the duplicated-trusted-voice device that the benchmarks warn has been used twice already. It's more than a noun swap, but too close in its frame.

- possible giveaway, beat 5 to beat 9: dismissed. "A voice called for light" sets up the pattern, but the payoff (the voice is Gran's dead father, calling her name) isn't stated; it builds dread, not an early solution.
- possible giveaway, beat 5 to beat 8: dismissed. "Its light is always green" plants the green light, but beat 8's true starboard explanation makes it ambiguous. The viewer expects a green light (suspense) but can't settle what it is.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes. The hook (a sick gran, a strange promise) and the bell swinging beside a still flag hold attention, and every beat adds a new fact up to a real choice. The likeliest drop-off is the cluttered beat 1 (three voices before the rule lands) and the dense logbook reading in beats 5 and 6. Viewers who reach beat 9 and miss that Gran is Ellen will finish confused rather than chilled.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Close the rescue logic. Make the boat's problem that it's blind, not that it has no engine ("Our electrics are gone, no GPS, we're steering blind!"), have the coastguard say they can't raise the boat ("only you can help them"), and in beat 5 have you ring the ward on the wall phone ("Ellen's sedated, love. Ring in the morning."). This removes the top-comment holes and reinforces that Ellen is Gran. 2. Make the beat 9 payoff land for a cold or muted viewer: a readable wristband insert in beat 1, a cut to the beat 2 photo of the father as the radio says "It's Dad", and the geography fixed (do the climb first, so the call happens beside the switch at the top). 3. Rewrite the final cards around the real conflict, in parallel and with consequences (e.g. "If you switch it on, you do what her father did in 1979. If you keep it dark, four people hit the rocks you can't see."), and replace the stair-creak / "came up the steps" threat with a lighthouse-specific one, to move clear of Tower 9's lone-tower frame.
