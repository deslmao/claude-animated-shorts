# Independent evaluation: The First Surprise

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 4, 12
- evidence: Beat 1 is very clear: the man says "Mm. Lovely soup." while his shadow tips the bowl into a plant, and the line gives the rule. Beat 2 then packs three locations and three rules into one beat: "Interviews happen in the dark" (a black frame with two pairs of eyes), "surprise parties never work" (a door opens, light spills in, and the guests' shadows "leap up before the guests"), and "Unless you're completely lost in what you're doing" (a painter whose shadow copies her strokes). Beat 4's album shows "the guests' shadows visible in the doorway" in small photos.
- problem: Why surprise parties fail is the story's central problem, the thing the whole plan must beat, yet it gets a fraction of the densest beat. As staged, the failure happens at the reveal itself: the shadows leap a moment before the guests while the door opens. A cold viewer can't tell how the domino lamp in beat 12, where light also floods a room of guests at once, avoids that. The painter loophole, which the middle of the story depends on, is a "quick cut". The planned pictures and the facts the viewer needs are crowded into the same few seconds.
- fix: Split beat 2. Give the party failure its own clearly ordered shot that shows the leak happening before the reveal: the birthday man, still in the lit hallway, sees the crouching guests' shadows jump on the wall or frosted glass, rolls his eyes, and only then opens the door. Hold the painter shot for at least 1.5 s, so her shadow can be seen matching her stroke for stroke. Beats 10 and 12 then read as the direct answer: no light reaches the guests until the instant the lamp fires.

### recontextualisation
- result: weak
- beats: 3, 4, 7, 12
- evidence: The emotional reveal, that Gus secretly wants a surprise, is given away in beat 3 (his shadow claps at the TV). Beat 12 calls this back ("claps the way it clapped at the TV"). The domino trick is planted openly in beat 7 (the book over the foot switch), so beat 11 confirms what we expected rather than reframing it.
- problem: Nothing at the end makes an earlier image mean something new. The ending is a warm callback that confirms what the viewer already knew. It is satisfying, but it isn't the "oh" that drives rewatches and comments.
- fix: Use the rule itself for a reframe. In the album (4), show Gus's face smiling politely in every photo while only his shadow slumps, so his face and his shadow never match. At the payoff (12), hold one shot on the wall where, for the first time in the film, Gus and his shadow do exactly the same thing: both arms up. Viewers then reread every earlier Gus moment (the scoff, the polite smiles) as eighty years of hiding. Optionally echo the line: a teary "Silly." in place of or before "You got me."

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the whole plot is beating a rule.
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 4, 9, 12
- finding: Top-comment probes. (a) "Why does a lamp coming on work, when in beat 2 the shadows leap before the guests anyway?" The beat-2 staging puts the failure at the moment of reveal, so the domino solution doesn't visibly fix it (see the clarity fix). (b) "Eighty years and nobody thought to keep the hallway dark or get the birthday person absorbed in something?" This is partly answered if beat 2 shows the leak happening before the door opens, and if past failures are shown to come from the organiser's own shadow (as Milo's nearly does). (c) "The cake box was sitting on the kitchen counter the whole time" (9): Gus could see it. Hide the cake or have Milo glance at a clock instead. Smaller gaps that are fine: Gus's "Bit dark in here?" is answered in character; guests arriving by the back door is covered by the text.

### consistency

- result: pass
- beats: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13
- finding: The TV clap (3) matches the final clap (12). The hugging shadow (3) matches the shadow hug (13). The domino-sized gap (10) matches the run going through the gap (11). "Lights OFF" and the curtains (7) match the dark living room (10, 12). Shadows acting first (13: the shadows hug, then the people) matches the rule. ### reproduction
- applies: yes, it has declared inspirations and a structural resemblance to a benchmark.
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 2, 8, 9, 11, 12
- finding: The closest source is *In This Town, You Get 1,000,000 Steps*. Sequence: both have a rule, comic proofs and a planted loophole that is used later, but the events differ (a party plan, two near-leaks, a domino run, not a runaway stroller and a countdown). Motivation: a gift of love, not an emergency rescue. Conflict: keeping a secret, not a scarce resource. Solution: darkness, absorption and a self-triggered chain reaction, not walking on hands. Payoff meaning: an old man's hidden longing fulfilled, not heroism and a town-wide ripple. Peter Pan and Liar Liar contribute only a concept and a trope. No distinctive sequence is reproduced.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes, the soup gag in beat 1 is an instant hook, and the dramatic irony of Milo's leaking shadow keeps tension through the middle. The likeliest drop-off is the crowded rule montage in beat 2 and the expository album in beat 4.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Re-stage and split beat 2 so the party failure clearly happens before the reveal (the guests' shadows leap on the hallway wall or glass while the birthday man is still outside), and hold the painter-loophole shot long enough to read. This makes the climax visibly the answer to the rule. 2. Add a real reframe: in the album, show Gus's polite smile never matching his slumped shadow; at the payoff, hold the first moment in the film where Gus and his shadow do exactly the same thing. 3. Close the small gaps the comments will find: hide the cake box (or have Milo glance at a clock), and mark the jump from breakfast to about 6 PM in beat 8 (a dusk window or a clock at 5:58).
