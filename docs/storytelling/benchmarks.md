# Storytelling benchmarks: what each reference teaches

**Sources:** the owner's research brief (12 Nov 2026) and our own productions. The external videos' transcripts are not in the
repo, so these notes cover only the *mechanisms* described in the brief. They are studied for technique only: never copy
a plot, character or scene. Factual claims in viral videos (and in their comments) are **unverified** until
`research.md` sources them.

| # | Benchmark | Format | What makes the opening work | How it progresses | What the viewer must understand | What the visuals must communicate | What the payoff achieves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Float Away** (ours) | emotional + world-rule | An absurd rule with a loved person at risk: Grandpa is floating away. | Small signs of Gus getting lighter, then comic attempts, then the cost to the relationship, then the realisation. | The rule (attention = weight), and that Milo is part of the cause. | The physical lightness (feet leaving the ground) and the distance between Milo and Gus. | **Recontextualisation:** "He started floating the spring I stopped visiting. I was the last one." The earlier comedy turns into regret, then repair. |
| 2 | **1 Million Steps** | world-rule | One countable rule that changes ordinary life. | The rule's everyday consequences (children carried in wagons), then the hero's personal cost (a runner with few steps left), then an emergency. | Exactly what the counter counts. | The counter, the wagons, the handstand set up early. | **A clever earned solution:** the rule counts *feet*, so the handstand, set up earlier, saves his sister. |
| 3 | **The Last Stop** | immersive | Second person, a specific wrong situation, and a reason to fear it: the driver says skipping your stop is why you're alive. | Two sources (the driver, and a text from "Mum" saying it isn't Mr. Hale). Each new clue (the car behind the bus, the headcount on a clipboard) supports one side and makes the choice harder. | Who is saying what, and that both could be lying. | The POV of the driver watching you, the texts on the phone, the car behind, the clipboard. | **A genuine dilemma:** trust the driver or "Mum". The viewer has already chosen in their head. |
| 4 | **The Ancient Jar** (facts unverified) | action-first | An irrational act with stakes: a bronze ball in a dragon's mouth, "if it falls, run." | Doubt, then the circumstances, then the turn, then the mechanism (a pendulum tips, the ball drops toward the direction of the quake), then proof. | How motion becomes a falling ball. | Animate the jar, the dragons, the pendulum and the ball dropping; never just a narrator describing it. | **The strangeness dissolves into ingenuity.** Accuracy matters: the comments disputed the historical account, so source it or word it as uncertain. |
| 5 | **The Empty Classroom** (our reference story) | immersive + mystery | A forbidden place with a time rule ("after 6 PM"). | Escalating evidence inside the room, personal involvement, then contradictions between what's seen and what's said. | The rule, and every clue as it appears. | Each clue big and held: the room, objects moved, writing. | **A reveal that reuses the earlier clues**, so the viewer re-reads the story. |
| 6 | **Everyone Ran. Nobody Woke You.** | immersive + mystery | You wake into the aftermath: everyone escaped, and you were left. | Environmental evidence (flickering lights, rules written on glass, sounds out of view), then tally marks revealing earlier attempts, then the seventh line. | That the marks are *yours*, so you've done this before. | The glass, the marks, the corridor and doors showing the threat moving closer; the sound off-screen. | **A forced interpretation** of the final mark. The sequel recontextualises: it was following you home. |
| 7 | **We All Live on One Ship** | world-rule + emotional | A world, a threat and a rule in one line: the ship must never stop, because something behind it drinks the ocean. | Daily life under the rule, then the boy's fear of the stern, then his father can't steer, so the boy must face it. | Why stopping is fatal, and why he fears the back of the ship. | The wake, the stern and the thing behind; the boy's face when he's near the stern. | **The fear set up earlier becomes the climax.** He confronts it: character change as payoff. |
| 8 | **The Job Nobody Wants in Japan** (facts to verify) | action-first | A contradiction: paid to do nothing. | Show what the job *feels* like before explaining it, then reveal the mechanism (why a company would want it). | The incentive that makes it rational. | The empty desk, the clock, the person's daily routine. | **Delayed explanation:** the odd situation becomes logical. Verify the claims independently. |
| 9 | **Would You Trust the Phone or the Door?** | immersive + mystery | A message from your future self, while someone with your voice is at the door. | Two threats, each with evidence. A photo reveals the sender was already inside the room. | Which source claims what. | The messages, the door, the photo. | **The evidence collapses one assumption**, and the dilemma gets sharper, not resolved. |
| 10 | **The Numbers** (technique only; don't reproduce its plot) | serial / character | Characters with a long plan and relationships that matter. | Long-term planning, then trust, then betrayal, then a payoff that was planted. | Who wants what, and who knows what. | Relationship staging: who stands with whom, what's hidden, who sees it. | **The betrayal and payoff are earned by setups** across the story. |
| 11 | **Storm oil** (Rick's-Clips style; facts unverified) | action-first | An odd act at sea (pouring oil on a storm). | Sailors doubt it, then the storm, then the turn, then the mechanism (a surface film damping small waves), then a visible result. | What the oil actually changes, and its limits. | The film spreading, the small ripples flattening, the contrast with an untreated patch. | **A mechanism shown, not told.** Must be sourced: the dramatic claims may overstate the effect. |

## Our other benchmarks
- **Pirate Democracy:** competing characters, a vote, consequences. The vote *is* the story event, so hands must go up and the count must be seen.
- **Every Lie:** one visible rule (a sign appears over each lie), escalating gags, and a reversal on the narrator himself.

## What differs between them
Each benchmark has a different engine:
- **Relationship plus realisation:** 1, 7.
- **Rule plus earned trick:** 2.
- **Two sources plus evidence plus decision:** 3, 9.
- **Odd act plus mechanism:** 4, 8, 11.
- **Clues plus recontextualising reveal:** 5, 6.
- **Long setups plus betrayal:** 10.

The selector in docs/STORYTELLING.md §1 picks among these; it doesn't average them into one formula.
