# Writer lessons: the writer's own recurring mistakes

Read this before writing any story. Each lesson comes from an independent evaluation of a real sample (Nov 2026);
the fix is the habit that prevents it.

1. **Back-filled logic leaves plot holes.**
   - **Examples:** a drifting boat that a light can't save (Keeper's Rule v2); a lamp that was dark for no reason (v1); office workers in a tower that might topple, with no reason given (Citicorp v1); "why not just whistle or run after her?" (100 Words v3).
   - **Habit:** write the **Constraints** and the **Easy outs** before the twist, and ask "why can't they just…?" for every obstacle.
2. **Benchmark gravity.** The nearest successful example pulls the plot towards its own sequence.
   - **Examples:** Keeper's Rule turned into Tower 9 with the nouns changed; 100 Words twice followed the *1,000,000 Steps* skeleton, and its premise surface matches the novel *Vox*.
   - **Habit:** *declare* the inspirations in References and compare stage by stage. Inspiration is fine; reproducing a source's sequence isn't. Write three genuinely different directions before choosing.
3. **Facts bent to fit the drama.**
   - **Examples:** "cheap bolts" (the real defect was too few bolts, because the braces were classed as trusses); "nobody could have known" (NIST says the data should have led them there); the order of events reversed.
   - **Habit:** build the beat from the **claim**, not the claim from the beat. Every factual line cites a claim with its source and a quote.
4. **Information carried by narration, not pictures.**
   - **Examples:** "Ellen" planted only in a nurse's line; clues in handwriting nobody can read in time; redundant narration flagged in all six evaluations.
   - **Habit:** give each event its **visual proof** before its line. A line adds meaning; it doesn't describe the shot.
5. **Overloaded beats.**
   - **Examples:** six actions in one silent beat (100 Words beat 8); the church, braces, bolts, corners and damper in four beats (Citicorp).
   - **Habit:** one new idea at a time. A continuous sequence may carry several lines, but each change must be readable at Shorts pace.
6. **Stakes stated, not shown.**
   - **Examples:** "could finish his career" said once; a broken promise with no visible cost.
   - **Habit:** **show what they lose** before the climax, and give staying silent or failing real weight.
7. **Patch revisions create new holes.**
   - **Example:** fixing the dilemma's balance created the drifting-boat hole.
   - **Habit:** revise the **spine first**, then re-check the whole chain.
8. **One idea, no alternatives.** The first idea's flaws get baked in.
   - **Habit:** write three directions that differ in conflict, decision, solution or ending.
9. **Solutions decided by chance.**
   - **Example:** the hurricane turning away settled the climax, not the protagonist's choice (Citicorp: his decision to weld the critical joints first is what made the tower safe).
   - **Habit:** the protagonist's own decision must cause the resolution.
10. **A defensible ending line.** Top comments attack quotable lines and overclaims.
    - **Example:** "Nobody could have known."
    - **Habit:** end on a line that's true and earned.
11. **Prop and costume continuity breaks during revisions.**
    - **Example:** who wears which boots. Ivy leaves in your boots, you chase her in trainers, yet the ending shows "one big trail and one small", and her boots are "soaked" though she never wore them (One Set of Footprints).
    - **Habit:** for every object the plot depends on, track where it is and who has it at each event, and re-check it after any revision.
12. **Narration undoing a planted clue.**
    - **Example:** after phase 1 said "don't linger on the lump in the bed", the writer added the line "Ivy's asleep. At least, that's what it looks like", announcing the trick anyway.
    - **Habit:** a plant must look ordinary. Never add a line that tells the viewer to doubt it.
