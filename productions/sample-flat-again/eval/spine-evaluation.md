# Independent evaluation: Flat Again
packet: ee7a0ec6

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 3, 4, 10
- evidence: E2 ends on "Mr. Shah holding up one finger", E3 has Grandad "curls two fingers on the table, again and again", and E4 has "Mr. Shah's two fingers". E3 also has to land the padlock, the two keys, the spare on the hook by Grandad's door, the pill pots in a row, the "B... b...", the pointing at the shed, Mum's motive line and the earbuds, all in one beat. E10 says he has been trying to tell you "since Sunday", but no event takes place on Sunday.
- problem: Grandad's key gesture sits between two finger-counts, so a first-time viewer will most likely read his "two fingers" as a count ("two lates?") and not as a squeeze on a brake lever. The clue that is meant to carry the payoff gets misfiled. E3 is the densest beat in the film and holds three of the four clues, so some of them will be lost on a first watch. "Since Sunday" points at something the viewer never saw.
- fix: Keep the two kinds of fingers apart. Have Mr. Shah count lates as tally marks (chalk on the shop door), or have Grandad's gesture be unmistakably a grip: his good hand closing round an imaginary handlebar, then squeezing. Split E3 into the padlock and keys on one side and Grandad's attempt on the other (the dinner table, a moment later). Change "since Sunday" to an event the viewer sees (see consistency).

### cause and effect
- result: pass
- beats: 1-13
- evidence: Each link follows from the one before. The flat tyres (E1) make you pump up and ride, which brings the late start and the first count (E2). That leads to the padlock (E3). The padlock is beaten and the pump is taken (E4), so the culprit must be in the house (E5). That leads to the stakeout (E6), which clears Mum by the 4:30 car and the key still on its hook, and then to catching Grandad (E7, E8), the demonstration (E9), the realisation (E10), the repair together (E11) and the clean stop (E12). The escalation is real: lates 1, then 2, then last chance, and the saboteur escalates too (tyres only, then the pump as well).
- problem: The only weak link is the time it takes (E11 to E12), which is covered under consistency. Nobody is rescued by luck. The stop at E12 is earned by the new cable.
- fix: none needed beyond the timeline fix.

### emotional investment
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 8, 13
- evidence: Before the climax, Grandad is shown only through his disability (pill pots, a stick, broken words) and a dusty RAY'S CYCLES box. The protagonist's tie to him is shown only negatively ("You put your earbuds in"). The paper round's value is shown only as Mr. Shah's count and Dex's grin. E13's "two-finger tap on your helmet" reads as a ritual, but the helmet and the ritual never appear before.
- problem: The ending ("he was protecting me, and I ignored him") lands only if we already care about Grandad and about what was lost between you. As planned, the viewer meets him as an obstacle and a suspect, and the warmth arrives only after the reveal. Why a thirteen-year-old cares so much about this round is never shown, so the first half's stakes feel generic.
- fix: Add one pre-stroke image of the bond in E1, under the torch, for example the bike's frame carrying a RAY'S CYCLES sticker (he built it for you), or a photo on the shed wall of him holding the seat of your first bike, tapping two fingers on your helmet. E13's tap then becomes a returned ritual, not a new one. Give the round one concrete want, such as a coin jar or a savings card with a goal on it, seen once in E1 or E3.

### setup and payoff
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 9, 10, 11, 13
- evidence: The setups are visible and fair. The neat caps "side by side ... exactly on its edge" (E1, E4) rhyme with the pill pots "side by side in a row" (E3), pointing to who. The "B... b...", the curling fingers and the pointing at the shed (E3) point to why. The silver strands bristling at the lever (E2) point to what. The RAY'S CYCLES box (E1) is the solution (E11). The earbuds in (E3) pay off as earbuds down (E10). The tartan gown (E3) identifies the hand (E7). Nothing is pulled from nowhere: the new cable comes from a box seen in the first beat.
- problem: There are two small risks. First, the E2 close-up of strands bristling right after a near-miss at the junction is loud. Attentive viewers may solve the "why" before the second late, and then the stakeout (E6-E7) only confirms it. Second, E13's helmet tap has no setup (see emotional investment).
- fix: Keep the strands, but in the background of the shot (behind the hand, not the subject of the shot), so they read on a rewatch. Set up the helmet tap as described above.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes. A grandad who can't speak sabotages a bike instead of warning anyone directly, so the method has to survive "why didn't he just...?"
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 3, 4, 8, 9
- finding: "He can't write" and "his right hand can't grip" pre-empt the note and the do-it-yourself repair. But E9 shows the means he had all along: take your hand, put it on the lever and squeeze. He can walk (slowly, with a stick), and he can reach the shed (E7, E8), so nothing stopped him from doing on Monday evening what he does on Wednesday. In E3 he only points from the table, and Mum (who is present and an adult) is never physically shown the problem. The top-comment probe is "He could've just walked you to the shed and squeezed the brake on day one." Smaller probes: why the spare key is hung right by Grandad's door (convenient), and why you shrug off Monday's near-miss at the junction without checking the brake. The pump in his pocket on Wednesday (E8) is acceptable, since he is keeping it on him so you can't find it, but the viewer gets no hint of that. Fix: turn the hole into the emotional cost. In E3, show Grandad actually try. He gets up, takes your wrist and tugs you toward the shed. You pull away and put your earbuds in, and Mum steers him back to his chair ("Sit down, Dad, it's locked now"). Then nobody can say he didn't try the direct route. Hang the spare on the hook by the back door (which happens to be beside his room) so its position reads as household routine. In E2, give the protagonist a reason not to look at the brake: the clock, and Mr. Shah already counting.

### consistency
- applies: yes. The story runs on clock times and a three-day countdown.
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 11, 12
- finding: (1) The Wednesday timeline doesn't fit Monday's. On Monday you find the tyres at 5:40, pump them and still reach the shop at 6:12, so the trip takes about 25 minutes. On Tuesday the round on foot starts at 6:25. On Wednesday Grandad takes the key at 5:20, and then come the confrontation, the demonstration, the realisation, fitting a new cable with a stroke-limited guide, and pumping two tyres. You still "ride down Gallows Hill" at 5:58 and are at the shop at 6:00. Either the ride takes two minutes (so Monday's 12-minute lateness makes no sense) or Wednesday is impossible. (2) "Since Sunday" (E10) refers to an event that is never shown, and the viewer never sees how or when Grandad found the fray. (3) Object continuity mostly holds. The spare key goes from the hook (E3, E5, E6) to the tartan hand (E7) to the padlock (E8), and the pump goes from the hook (E1, E2) to missing (E4) to his pocket (E8) to your hands (E11). The caps appear on the bench at E8 but have no E8 row in the objects table, the earbuds' presence in the shed at E10 is not tracked, and the helmet (E13) is never established. Fix: move Grandad's arrival to about 4:50, after Mum's 4:30 car, which keeps her alibi, and have the clock read about 5:35 as you wheel the bike out, so the ride matches Monday's. Either show the discovery (Sunday evening, Grandad in the shed doorway, his eyes on the lever as you squeeze it to park) or change the reference to Monday. Add the E8 caps row and the earbuds and helmet to the table.

### dilemma balance
- applies: yes. There is no on-screen vote, but the premise sets up competing explanations (Dex or Mum wants you to lose the round, or someone is keeping you off the bike), and the viewer weighs suspects through E7.
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7
- finding: The evidence at the final beat before the reveal (E7) is lopsided. Against Dex there is only a grin and "Enjoy it while it lasts" (E2), and he is ruled out at E5 by checkable logic (the locked shed, a key only someone in the house can reach). That is good, but it removes him early. Against Mum there is one spoken motive line (E3), the fact that she fitted the lock, and the fact that she is up before 5:40. She is cleared at E6 by checkable evidence (the car leaves and the key stays), which is a strong, fair beat. Against Grandad there are the caps that rhyme with his pill pots, the gestures and the pointing, the hook by his door, a mechanic's skill and the tartan gown. Grandad clearly wins for anyone paying attention by E3 to E5. Because the real mystery is why, an obvious who is tolerable, but the stakeout then has little doubt to resolve. Fix: give Mum one piece of physical evidence that genuinely fits both readings, shown in E4 or E5. For example, show that her 4:30 route takes her past the hook and the shed, or show her quietly resetting your alarm "so you can sleep". The E6 alibi then removes a real suspect.

### reproduction
- applies: yes. Concepts are declared from The Last Stop and To Kill a Mockingbird.
- result: pass
- beats: 1-13
- finding: Against The Last Stop: the sequence differs (no vehicle journey, no rival sources by text, no head count). So do the motivation (a disabled grandad who cannot speak, against a driver who won't explain), the conflict (keeping a job against a saboteur in the house), the solution (repairing the bike together) and the payoff's meaning (communication across disability, and your own dismissiveness). Only the abstract inversion is shared. Against Boo Radley: only the concept of a misread protector is shared, with no shared scenes or characters. The closest folk shape is the "someone kept sabotaging my car and it saved my life" anecdote, but that is a trope, not a distinctive work. There is no close reproduction.

- result: pass

### cold viewer
- applies: yes
- result: weak
- beats: 1-13
- finding: A first-time viewer would think: "Someone kept letting my bike tyres down so I'd lose my paper round, so I locked the shed, staked out the spare key, and caught Grandad doing it. He couldn't talk, so he showed me the brake cable was about to snap on the hill; he'd been protecting me, and we fixed it together and I made it on time." The likely misreadings are these. The curling fingers will be read as a count, not a squeeze, until E9. Some viewers will think the brake already half-failed on Monday's near-miss. The ending raises "why didn't he just show me on Monday?" and "how did I fix it and get there in two minutes?". Viewers will also wonder where Sunday came from.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes. The hook is strong (two flat tyres, no puncture, the caps lined up neatly) and the count of lates creates real pressure. The risk point is E5 to E6: a viewer who has already linked the caps to the pill pots and the strands to the "B..." may find the stakeout only confirms what they know. The demonstration (E9) and the repair together (E11) are the emotional reward and should hold them.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Close the "why didn't he just show you?" hole. In E3, have Grandad physically try: he takes your wrist and tugs you toward the shed, you pull away and put your earbuds in, and Mum steers him back. Show (or re-time) how he discovered the fray, replacing the unseen "since Sunday". 2. Repair the Wednesday timeline. Bring Grandad in at about 4:50 (after Mum's 4:30 alibi) and have the bike leave at about 5:35, so the 6:00 arrival matches Monday's ride. Add the missing E8 caps row and the earbuds and helmet to the objects table. 3. Make the key clue and the bond read. Separate Grandad's squeeze gesture from Mr. Shah's finger-count (tally marks for the lates, a grip-and-squeeze for Grandad), and add one early image of the pre-stroke bond (a bike he built, the two-finger helmet tap) so we care before E8 and E13 pays off a ritual we've seen. Optionally, give Mum one fair-play piece of evidence so the stakeout clears a real suspect.
