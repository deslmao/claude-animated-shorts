# Independent evaluation: Locked Out
packet: 53e034eb

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 8, 10, 12, 15
- evidence: Beat 1 gives who, where and the oddity in one line ("a man is paying me double to break into his own house"), with the job card (LOCKOUT · 9 ALDER ROW · 02:14) and the stake (the FINAL WARNING letter on the visor). Beat 3 turns the letter into a pressure the viewer understands ("I can't afford to turn away the wrong man twice", with a glance back at the visor). Beat 8 names the turn in plain words ("It's one of ours. / She changed the lock yesterday. And kept the keys from him."). Beat 10 names the new situation and the clock ("She's upstairs with her little boy. Officers are four minutes out."). Beat 12 states the plan before it happens ("He still thinks I'm his way in. While he does, he'll wait at the door."), and "Do not open her door." / "I won't." confirms he's going back without opening it. At every point a first-time viewer can say who is where, what's happening and what's at stake. Phase 1 I3(1), the lie "at her mother's" against "everything he told me was true", is gone: every line Marsh says is true from where he stands ("She won't come down", "Just stopped working tonight", "It's my house!"), so beat 9's "Everything he's told me is true" holds and is the story's best line.
- problem: Minor only. Beat 1 asks the viewer to read a 12-word letter in an insert while hearing a different sentence. If they miss it, beat 3's "wrong man twice" still explains it, so this is a density cost, not confusion. Beat 15's positioning is covered under visual communication.
- fix: none required. Optional: shorten the visor letter to "FINAL WARNING: ONE MORE WRONGFUL REFUSAL = DISMISSAL" so it reads in one glance.
### viewer participation
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12
- evidence: Beats 3 to 7 give the viewer evidence to weigh themselves before the narrator interprets it: the licence matches the brass 9 and the bell (for him), the spaniel's tail thumps at his voice and the drawing shows DADDY in the same green coat (for him), but there's cash, "no call to the office", the finger to the lips (against him), and then a bright new cylinder in old paint, a paler outline of a bigger lock and fresh filings on the mat (beat 6). A sharp viewer reads "someone changed this lock today" from the macro shot before the narrator says "New locks don't just stop working", and the light going out (beat 7) lets them guess who. That is the Tower 9 technique of a fact the viewer can check, used well. After the turn the participation becomes "what would I do?": the hand over the horn (beat 11), with its argument against, and the decision to leave the locked van (beat 12).
- problem: none.
- fix: none.
### cause and effect
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17
- evidence: The chain is now causal end to end. Marsh wants it quiet (beat 2), so he needs the locksmith rather than the brick. The locksmith leaves (beat 9), so Marsh falls back on the window (beat 11). The locksmith returns with "Found them!", so Marsh drops the brick and goes back to the door (beat 13). The fake picking is noticed ("You're not even trying"), which forces the ultimatum (beat 14). The refusal is shouted, so windows light up and a neighbour comes out threatening police (beats 15, 16), so Marsh, whose whole method depended on a sleeping street, lets go and walks past the brick to the road (beat 16), where the officers meet him (beat 17). Phase 1 I1, "the title act doesn't cause the rescue", is fixed. The jammed lock is gone. The protagonist's two choices (go back and bluff; refuse loudly) are what keep the glass intact until the police come, and both are shown having an effect (the brick dropped; the grip opening at the lit street). The "he had a brick the whole time" comment now has a visible answer: he did pick it up, and he put it down because the quiet way seemed to be back.
- problem: Residual. The police arrive exactly at 00:00 on the operator's estimate. That's a countdown convention and acceptable, but it means the arrest itself is scheduled, not caused. The protagonist's job was to make the four minutes hold, and he does.
- fix: none required.

### recontextualisation

### visual communication
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 8, 15, 18
- evidence: Most of it reads muted: the new cylinder in flaking paint with filings (beat 6), the brick at the glass and the hand over the horn (beat 11), the phone hidden screen-in and the cuff pulled over the watch (beat 12), the brick dropping as he returns (beat 13), the pick resting beside the keyway rather than in it (beat 14), window after window lighting and the grip opening (beat 16), the blue light (beat 17). But the climax depends on one spatial fact the planned camera can't show. Beat 15 is "pov (rising, the brick and the bay window behind me, him at the door)". In first person, what's behind me isn't in frame. The viewer sees Marsh at the door and then a wide of the street, but never sees that the locksmith has put himself between Marsh and the brick and the glass. That positioning is what makes the shout safe when the horn in beat 11 was argued to be dangerous. Two readable-text inserts also carry plot at Shorts speed: the 12-word visor letter (beat 1) and the History entry (beat 8, rescued by the spoken line). In beat 18 the hand stopping at the letterbox flap is a nice beat with sound but ambiguous muted.
- problem: Without the blocking, the viewer meets a contradiction in beat 15: "he just told us noise would make him smash the window, and now he's shouting?" The shout then reads as a loss of temper instead of a decision timed and placed by the watch.
- fix: In beat 15, after the watch insert (01:30), cut to one over-the-shoulder or low wide from beside the bay window showing me stepping onto the path with the brick at my feet and the glass behind me, Marsh at his door. Or have me pick up the brick as I stand, so the muted viewer sees the danger taken out of his reach before I shout. Optionally add a short thought before the shout ("Ninety seconds. And he has to go through me now.").

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, a protagonist who disobeys "stay in your vehicle" and an antagonist with a brick invite "why didn't he just…" questions.
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 8, 10, 11, 12, 15, 16, 17
- finding: Phase 1 I2 is largely resolved. (1) The app no longer says "DO NOT OPEN" while booking the job. The History only records a lock change for R. Marsh, and the narrator finds it himself by tapping one level deeper, so "the app told him and sent him anyway" is gone. A smaller probe remains ("his own firm changed that lock yesterday and nothing flagged it?"), which is plausible enough for a night job. (2) The live call is pocketed screen-in and the watch is covered before he goes back (beat 12), so the device is no longer in Marsh's view. (3) The horn is now weighed on screen and in a line (beat 11). (4) "No paperwork" became "No invoice, no call to the office", which fits an app-booked job where Marsh wants cash and no check-in. (5) The jammed lock is gone. What remains: (a) **Horn vs shout.** In beat 11 noise is the reason not to act, and in beat 15 noise is the solution. The story's answer is that by then he stands between Marsh and the glass with 90 seconds left, and he soaks up the grapple while the street wakes, but that answer depends on blocking the POV can't show (see visual communication). (b) **The operator's line.** "Do not open her door" answers "Stay on the line", not his leaving the van. An operator who just said "Stay in your vehicle" would repeat that. It works for the viewer (it clarifies his intent) but sounds slightly off. (c) Four-minute police at 2 a.m. is optimistic and will get a joke or two. Top-comment probe: the most-liked nitpick is likely "Why not just honk? The neighbours waking up is literally what scared him off." Fix (a) and it has a visible answer.

## Overall

Beat 6's macro of the new lock and beat 8's "It's one of ours" is where a viewer leans in, and the brick at the glass with the hand over the horn (beat 11), then the countdown under the cuff, hold them through the climax. The ending image (the family at the lit window) and the closing line give a reason to comment. Top comment: "'I checked who he was. I never asked who the lock was for.' Give this man a raise, and if they fire him at that review…" Top critical comment: "Why not just honk the horn? The street waking up is literally what made him run."
