## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 8, 10, 15
- evidence: Beat 1 gives the whole situation in one line ("a man is paying me double to break into his own house") over the job card LOCKOUT · 9 ALDER ROW · 02:14. Who (a locksmith, his van, his picks), where (9 Alder Row at night), what (a lockout) and the stake (FINAL WARNING: WRONGFUL REFUSAL OF SERVICE, then "One more complaint and I'm finished" in beat 3) are all in place by about 0:12. "She" and "R. Marsh" read as his wife without explanation, Biscuit is obviously the dog, and the operator's line in beat 10 states the new rule (four minutes, stay in the vehicle) before the stall uses it. Two things carry a small risk but don't fail: (1) beat 15 packs a shove, the key at the stub, clawing, being hauled up, the watch, the run for the brick, blue light, the arrest and the brick dropping into one beat, so it has to be given the screen time it needs or it will blur. (2) The narrator speaks both aloud ("Just checking the lock model.", "Wrong picks.", "Found them! One minute.") and in his head ("Someone's awake up there.", "A horn, and that window's gone.", "Four minutes. So I pretend."). If the two don't sound different, beat 7's "Someone's awake up there" sounds as if it was said to Marsh, and his "Problem?" reads as an answer to it rather than to the torch tilting.
- problem: none at story level. The two risks above are production notes.
- fix: none required. In production, give inner narration and spoken lines different treatment (closer, drier mix for inner lines), and give beat 15 enough frames for each action.
### viewer participation
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 13
- evidence: The viewer can check the evidence themselves and weigh it both ways. Against him: cash, "no call to the office" and the finger to his lips (2); a bright new cylinder in flaking old paint, with the outline of a bigger lock and filings on the mat (6); the upstairs light, visible since beat 1, going out (7). For him: the licence held beside the brass 9 and the MARSH bell (3), the spaniel's tail thumping at his voice, and DADDY in a green coat in the child's drawing (5). Most viewers will have guessed "she changed the locks on him" from the picture in beat 6, before the narrator confirms it in beat 8, and that feels earned. The second half gives them a readable clock (the stopwatch, 01:30, 02:20, 02:40, 03:50) to count against "four minutes", and a real "what would I do?" moment: stay locked in the van as told, sound the horn, or go back out and lie.
- problem: none
- fix: none
### cause and effect
- result: weak
- beats: 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15
- evidence: Phase-1 finding I1, partly resolved. The chain up to the climax is now caused by the protagonist. His walk to the van (9) is why Marsh goes for the brick (11). He rejects the horn on screen ("A horn, and that window's gone"), and his lie "Found them! One minute" is why Marsh drops the brick and comes back (12). That stall is visibly his doing. The snap (14) now has a reason the viewer can see: it answers "Open it. Now." and the hand gripping his shoulder (13), so he can't be forced to open the door. But the viewer still meets the core of I1. The snap closes the door at 02:40 and leaves the window, which Marsh has already shown he will use, brick in hand, three beats earlier. He reaches it only because he spends 70 seconds clawing at a stub he knows was sabotaged ("What did you do?"), and the police arrive "as he straightens with it". The family is saved by the man's rage and by the clock, to the second, not by the title act. A logical viewer can argue that the snap made him go for the window sooner.
- problem: The climactic decision doesn't visibly cause the rescue. The top critical comment writes itself: "He had a brick the whole time. Why'd he waste a minute on the lock?" or "Snapping the pick just sent him to the window."
- fix: Let the narrator's own choices close the window route too. In beat 12, as Marsh drops the brick by the path, my boot nudges it under the hedge (or I scoop it into the tool bag on the way past, in hands-in-frame pov). In beat 15, Marsh runs to where he dropped it, finds nothing and scrabbles in the border for another, and the blue light hits as he straightens with a new one. Now the snap holds the door, the hidden brick holds the window, and both are his doing. (Alternative: after the snap, say aloud "It's jammed. I'll have to drill it. Ninety seconds," so Marsh believes the quiet way still works and stays at the door.)
