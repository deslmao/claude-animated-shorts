## Research summary
Walking through a doorway can make you forget what you were about to do (the "doorway effect", or location updating
effect). In Gabriel Radvansky's experiments, people who carried objects through a doorway remembered them worse than
people who walked the same distance within one room, in virtual and real rooms. The explanation is the event
horizon model: the brain segments experience into events, and a doorway is an event boundary that "files away" the
previous episode, making what belonged to it harder to retrieve. The effect is not universal: a 2021 series of
experiments (McFadyen et al.) found no overall doorway effect and an impairment only when participants were also
under working-memory load.

## Script
1. You walk into a room and instantly forget why. Scientists think the doorway did it.
2. Meet Milo. He needs his keys.
3. He heads to the kitchen to grab them.
4. **milo:** Wait... why am I in here?
5. He checks the fridge, a drawer, even the toaster. The thought is just gone.
6. Psychologist Gabriel Radvansky put this to the test.
7. People carried objects through rooms, then were quizzed on what they were carrying.
8. Those who walked through a doorway forgot more than those who walked the same distance in one room.
9. **milo:** Wait, a door made me forget?
10. You see, your brain doesn't record life as one long video. It cuts it into scenes.
11. Psychologists call each cut an event boundary, and a doorway is a big one.
12. Walk through it, and your brain files the old scene away to make room for the new one.
13. Milo's plan for the keys got filed with the sofa.
14. It doesn't happen every time. Later studies found it mostly when people's minds were busy.
15. And going back can help, because the old room is full of cues.
16. So Milo walks back to the sofa... and remembers.
17. He grabs his keys, heads out the door... and forgets where he was going.
