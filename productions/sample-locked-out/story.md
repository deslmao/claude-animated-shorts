# Locked Out
spec: 3
format: mystery
perspective: first
why: A first-person mystery told through a locksmith's hands and trade knowledge: the viewer checks each piece of evidence with me (an ID, a dog, a drawing, a lock that's too new), is addressed by the man at the door, and reaches the decision at the same moment I do.
length: 100
voice: liamQuiet
voices: me=liam, marsh=brian, operator=alice, woman=jessica, neighbour=chris
mood: tense
factual: no

## Premise
- question: At 2:14 a.m. a man is paying me double to open his own front door, quietly. Is it really his door, and why has his key stopped working?
- protagonist: me, a night-call locksmith on a final written warning
- want: to do the job right, and keep the job
- fear: being wrong either way: opening a home to the wrong man, or leaving an honest man out in the cold again
- protects: whoever is behind the door
- decision: to go back and keep him at the front door until the police come; then, when he demands I open it, to refuse him out loud, where the whole street can hear
- loses: my job, if I refuse a man with valid ID (shown: the final warning on my van's visor, and the refusal logged at the end); her and her son's safety, if I open
- obstacle: every check he passes is genuine. The truth isn't a lie he tells; it's something he leaves out
- rule: none
- conflict: A, he's an honest man locked out of his own home after a row; B, he's not meant to be let in, and I'm the way in
- action: none

## Spine
depth: deep

### Constraints
- Kestrel Locks is the only 24-hour locksmith in town. At night, jobs are booked automatically through its app: no dispatcher is awake. Earlier jobs at an address sit one tap deeper, under History. A refused job is logged for review.
- Company rule: proof of address before I touch a lock. A driving licence counts.
- The final warning on my van's visor: last winter I refused a man with no ID, it was his house, and one more wrongful refusal means my job.
- A driving licence keeps its old address until its holder changes it.
- Kestrel's cylinders carry a small KL stamp on their face.
- At night the police are about four minutes away.
- The street is asleep. A locksmith opening a door looks legitimate to anyone who wakes; a man breaking a window, or fighting on a doorstep, doesn't.
- My watch has a countdown timer. My phone fits in my jacket's breast pocket.

### Alternatives

| 1 | a man pays double to have his own front door opened at 2 a.m.: an honest man locked out, or someone she's keeping out? | go back and hold him at the door, then refuse him out loud, instead of opening it or driving away | everything he says is true; the lock is new because his partner had it changed yesterday, by my own firm, and kept the keys from him | her door stays locked; she tells me what she saw from upstairs; her light comes back on |
| 2 | on my fourth driving test the examiner keeps sending me off the test route: a trick, something sinister, or something wrong with him? | break the test's rules and follow his directions | every turn passed a blue hospital sign: he is having a heart attack and knows a panicking learner crashes | I fail the test and save him; he asks for me at the retake |
| 3 | on the last train a small girl travels alone with a note pinned to her coat, for a station that isn't on the map | stop the train at an unlit halt | it's a request stop only her grandmother still uses | a lantern on the platform |
- chosen: 1. Its hook carries situation, stake and question in one line; its reveal turns the evidence itself around (the proof that he belongs is the danger); and first person is what makes it work: my hands and my trade knowledge are the plot, and the climax is a decision made with my own tools. Direction 2 is guessable early and confined to a car; direction 3 is gentle, with low stakes.
