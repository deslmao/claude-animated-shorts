# Independent evaluation: Locked Out
packet: 8328c2ae

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E2, E6, E11, E13
- evidence: Who, where and what are fixed in the first event (job card LOCKOUT, 9 ALDER ROW, 02:07; the man on the step; the cash; the finger to his lips) and the stake is on screen (the warning letter on the visor). Each later turn has one readable image: the licence beside the brass 9 (E2), the bright cylinder in flaking paint (E4), the strip of light going out (E5), the stub flush in the keyway (E11), the chain and her eyes (E13). Two cautions that do not yet break it: E2 carries four things at once (the rule, the licence, the bell, and the last-winter backstory, which has no picture of its own beyond the letter seen in E1), and E6 asks the viewer to read a four-sentence phone screen that carries the entire turn of the story.
- problem: none that stops a first-time viewer following it
- fix: none required; when writing, let the visor letter itself carry the last-winter complaint (readable heading) so E2 is not narration-only, and cut the E6 screen to two short lines

### cause and effect
- result: weak
- beats: E6, E8, E11, E12
- evidence: E1 to E5 chain properly (rule, so licence; bell, so dog; kneel, so the wrong lock; torch on the curtain, so the light goes out). From E6 the chain loosens. E6: the History screen does not just give a fact, it gives the verdict and the instruction ("Do NOT open for him. Call police."), so E7's decision is the app's, not his. E8: the operator says "We've just had a call from inside that address", so officers were already coming and his 999 call changes nothing in the world except what he knows. E11 to E12: he snaps the pick, the man goes straight for the brick that was by the path all along, and the police arrive as he straightens up at 03:51.
- problem: The title act, jamming the lock, does not cause the rescue. The window was always available (he was already at it in E8), so the snap closes a route the man had already shown he would abandon, and the outcome is delivered by the four-minute schedule, not by the protagonist's choice. What does protect the family is the stall in E9 and E10; the climax itself is a gesture. A viewer will feel the timing rather than the decision, and a commenter will say "he had a brick the whole time".
- fix: Make the snap buy the last stretch, visibly, on the timer. Snap at about 02:40, straight after "Open it. Now.", then spend the remaining minute on the consequence: he rams his own key at the blocked keyway, claws at the stub, orders me to get it out, and only then goes for the brick as the street turns blue. And let E6 give evidence, not orders (see plot hole), so E7 is his own decision.

### emotional investment
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E3, E5, E9, E13
- evidence: The protagonist earns liking by conduct, not statement: he turns down double-rate cash to do it by the book (E1), rings the bell first "as I always do" (E3), and gets out of a locked van against the operator's instruction when the brick comes out (E9). The people behind the door are felt before they are seen: a child's drawing, small wellies, a dog that knows the man's voice (E3), then a light going out and a curtain moving (E5). By E10 the viewer knows a child is in that house and a frightened adult is hiding upstairs.
- problem: none at the level of caring; the weakness is in what he personally stands to lose at the climax (see emotional cost)
- fix: none required here

### setup and payoff
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E2, E3, E8, E9, E12, E14
- evidence: Most payoffs are planted and seen first. The licence that passes the check in E2 is the exact thing the History note is about. The four minutes given in E8 are paid by a visible timer (00:48, 01:50, 02:40, 03:51). The brick dropped by the path in E9 is the brick he runs for in E12. The child's drawing in E3 (DADDY in a green coat) is answered by the child's drawing in E14 (THE LOCK MAN), and the warning letter shown in E1 is what that drawing covers. Thin spots: the thumb-turn that makes E13 possible exists only in the rules and is never on screen before the door opens; the property that solves the climax (a broken pick blocks the lock from outside) is explained at the moment it is used; the cash fanned in E1 is never seen again; and the letter's threat (one more complaint means the job) is set up but never tested.
- problem: none serious enough to mark down; a snapped piece of steel in a keyhole explains itself
- fix: none required; cheap gains are her hand on the thumb-turn in E13 and one use of the cash later (he pushes it at me again when I stall)

### originality and adaptation
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E5, E6, E8, E11
- evidence: The declared borrowings are concepts and techniques, used at a distance from their sources: the door as the line between safe and unsafe, a lie exposed by a fact the viewer can see (the "empty" house whose light goes out), and a counted thing (the call timer). The story has its own point of view, which none of the benchmarks have: the protagonist is outside with the suspect and is the tool the suspect needs. Its best idea is its own: every check is genuine because he really is the father, so the truth is an omission. It needs no knowledge of any source. One surface overlap must go (see reproduction): the surname HALE.
- problem: none at story level
- fix: none required beyond the rename noted under reproduction

### accuracy
- result: n/a fiction
- beats: E2, E11, E13
- evidence: Fiction; no factual claims are presented as fact. The real-world mechanics it leans on are plausible: a licence does keep an old address until changed, and a thumb-turn cylinder can still be worked from inside with the outer keyway blocked. One overstatement to watch in the writing: "no key, no pick, not even me" (a locksmith can drill a cylinder), which is a comment-bait line, not an accuracy failure.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the plot depends on a chain of obstacles and on what the antagonist does not notice
- result: weak
- beats: E1, E6, E8, E9, E10, E11
- finding: (1) The app. The firm's own record for this address says "Do NOT open for him. Call police", yet the same app booked a lockout there the next night and showed none of it on the job card. The rule that covers this (no dispatcher at night, History one tap deeper) is in the rules list but not in any event, so the viewer never learns it, and the whole plot hangs on it. Top-comment probe: "The app literally said DO NOT OPEN and still sent him there." (2) The phone. He leaves a live 999 call face-up in an open bag at the door while the man stands over him (E9, E10). The man also had his shadow across the History screen in E6 and could see my lit face on a call through the windscreen in E8. Three chances to see it, no reaction; the device that gives the viewer the countdown is in the antagonist's plain view. (3) Simpler means. Locked in the van, the horn would wake a street the man needs asleep. The story has an answer (while he believes the quiet way still works he puts the brick down, which E9 shows), but nothing shows the protagonist weighing it. (4) "No paperwork" (E1) for a job he booked through an app that has already made a record. (5) The jammed lock leaves the window, covered under cause and effect.
- fix: (1) Make the History entry a fact, not a flag: "Yesterday. Emergency lock change. Customer: R. Hale. Keys to R. Hale only." He and the viewer put it together with the dead key, the new cylinder and the light, and the operator's line in E8 confirms it. That removes the hole and keeps the job at risk until E8. (2) Either hide the phone (screen-down under the bag flap, with the timer shown to the viewer only) or use it: he sees the 999 screen, and that is what turns "You said one minute" into the hand on the shoulder. (3) One image in E8: my hand over the horn, him raising the brick at the glass, my hand coming away. (4) Have him ask for "no invoice, no call to the office" instead.

### consistency
- result: weak
- applies: yes, the packet makes claims about earlier events and tracks objects
- beats: E3, E4, E5, E6, E7, E13
- finding: (1) E7 says "Everything he told me was true", and the premise says the truth "isn't a lie he tells". But in E3 he says "They're at her mother's", a plain lie that the light in E5 exposes. The story's own best idea and its own declared Tower 9 technique contradict each other. (2) The fitter's sticker is "on the door's edge beside the lock". The edge of a shut door is inside the frame and cannot be seen or thumbed; and my torch went over that lock closely in E4 without finding a sticker from my own firm. (3) The upstairs light enters the objects table only at E5, already going out. It must have been on since E1, on a street of "dark windows", beside a man saying nobody is home, and nobody noticed. (4) The thumb-turn is not in the objects table and is never seen, though E13 depends on it. (5) The licence goes into my gloved hand in E2 and is never tracked again. (6) The tool bag that holds the phone from E9 is not tracked: it has to come from the van in E9, which works, but should be shown. Timer arithmetic (00:48, 01:50, 02:40, 03:51 against "four minutes out") and the brick's three positions are consistent.
- fix: (1) Change his E3 line to something true and evasive ("She won't come down. Not at this hour.") or drop the claim that everything was true. (2) Put the sticker on the face of the new cylinder's collar or on the letterbox flap, found only when I lean in at E6. (3) Plant the strip of light in the E1 wide shot, unremarked, so E5 is a detail the viewer could have checked. (4) Show her hand on the thumb-turn in E13. (5) Have him take the licence back in E2, or hand it to an officer in E12.

### dilemma balance
- applies: yes, until E6 the viewer is weighing two explanations (thief or locked-out resident), though there is no on-screen choice
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E2, E3, E4, E5
- finding: At E5, the last beat before the answer, the observable evidence is even. For the honest resident: a licence with his photo and this address, HALE on the bell, a dog whose tail thumps at his voice, DADDY in his green coat on the hall wall, a worn key. For the intruder: cash and quiet at 2 a.m., a dog left behind by a family said to be away, a key that will not enter, a day-old cylinder with filings on the mat, a light that goes out. Neither side wins, and the answer is a third reading that keeps every clue true. That is the strongest part of the plot. Its one weakness is that the answer arrives as a written instruction rather than a deduction (see plot hole, fix 1).

### emotional cost
- applies: yes, the premise names what he loses on each side
- result: weak
- beats: E1, E2, E6, E10, E11, E14
- finding: The cost on his side is the job: one more upheld complaint. It is shown before the climax (E1, E2) and it does real work once, when he accepts the ID in E2. Then E6 cancels it: with a written "do not open, call police" from his own firm, no refusal can be a wrong refusal. From E6 to E13 doing the right thing costs him one pick. What remains at the climax is physical risk (a hand on his shoulder, a shove), which is real but was never the stake the story set up, and the cost to the family is carried by people unseen until E13. The letter is then covered in E14 without ever having been in danger.
- fix: Keep the job at risk when he chooses. With the E6 change above he walks to the van on a judgement, not an instruction, with the visor letter in front of him as he dials (E8): if he is wrong, this is the second complaint. Optionally let the man say it at E10 ("I'll have your job for this"), so the snap in E11 is made knowing the price.

### reproduction
- applies: yes, three benchmarks are declared and the genre is crowded
- result: weak
- beats: E1, E2, E5, E6, E8, E11, E13
- finding: Closest sources are Tower 9 and The Last Stop. Sequence: different (they sit inside and are approached; here the protagonist stands outside with the suspect and is asked to do the opening). Motivations: different (a tradesman's rule and his job against a father who wants back in). Central conflict: different (not "which voice is lying" but "every proof is real and still wrong"). Solution: its own (stall, then destroy his own ability to open the door). Meaning of the payoff: its own ("You didn't open it"; the job's rule justified). No sequence, scene or line is reproduced. But one listed distinctive element is: the benchmarks name "Mr. Hale" as distinctive expression of The Last Stop, not to be reproduced, and this story, which declares The Last Stop as an inspiration, calls its ambiguous man D. HALE and puts HALE on the bell and in the app. The 2:07 a.m. start also repeats the benchmarks' 4:07 and 3:07 to the minute digit.
- fix: Rename the family (any surname not in the benchmarks) and move the time off ":07".

### cold viewer
- applies: yes
- result: pass
- beats: E1, E6, E11, E12, E13, E14
- finding: What a first-time viewer would say happened: "A locksmith is called at 2 a.m. to let a man into his own house, then finds out the man's ex had the locks changed yesterday to keep him out and is hiding inside with their kid. He calls the police, stalls, breaks his pick in the lock so nobody can open it, and the police grab the man just as he goes for a brick." That matches the plot. Likely misreadings: for the two beats between E11 and E13 some will think he has trapped the family inside; some will assume his call brought the police (it was hers) and so miss that she was not passive; some will ask why the app sent him at all; and nobody will know at E14 whether he keeps his job, since the letter is covered, not withdrawn. None of these breaks the story.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes, the opening is a real hook (double pay, cash, do it quietly, at 2 a.m.), each of the first five events hands the viewer a clue for one side or the other, and the brick plus a visible timer hold the back half; a real viewer would want to see whether he opens the door. The risks are E2 (the last-winter backstory, told with no picture), E6 (the puzzle is answered outright at about 40 percent, by a paragraph on a phone, and viewers who came for "is he lying?" have nothing left to work out), and E14, where the story is over at "You didn't open it" and five more pictures follow; expect the swipe there unless the coda is cut to the drawing going over the letter.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. E6: make the History entry a fact, not a verdict and an order ("Yesterday. Emergency lock change. Customer: R. Hale. Keys to R. Hale only."), so he and the viewer deduce it, the operator's "a call from inside that address" confirms it in E8, the job stays at risk until then, and the "the app said DO NOT OPEN and still booked him" hole disappears. 2. E9 to E12: make the snapped pick cause the ending and fix the phone: snap at about 02:40, show the last minute lost to the jammed lock (his key, his nails at the stub, "get it out") before he goes for the brick as the blue arrives; and either hide the live 999 call from a man standing over it or make his seeing it the trigger for "Open it. Now." 3. Continuity and overlap: he must not lie outright if the story claims everything he said was true (replace "They're at her mother's"); move the fitter's sticker off the edge of a shut door; plant the upstairs light in E1 and show the thumb-turn in E13; rename HALE, which the benchmarks list as The Last Stop's distinctive expression.
