# Independent evaluation: Locked Out
packet: 2da39292

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16
- evidence: Who, where and what are set in beat 1 by the pictures, without any explanation: a van, the job card "LOCKOUT · 9 ALDER ROW · 02:14", and "paying me double to break into his own house". Together they make the narrator a locksmith. Beat 8 names the firm ("KL, Kestrel Locks, my firm") at the moment it matters. The key fact, R. Marsh against D. Marsh, is on screen (licence in beat 3, History in beat 8), and the line "She changed the lock yesterday. And kept the keys from him." makes sure nobody misses it. In beat 10 the operator says who "she" is and where she is ("upstairs with her little boy"). The stakes, his job, are readable on the visor in beat 1 and spoken in beat 3.
- problem: Minor, not blocking. (a) In beat 12 the operator's "Do not open that door." / "I won't." comes over a shot of my hand on the *van* door handle. Some viewers will hear "that door" as the van door and think he's lying to the operator. That's tolerable irony, but unintended if the writer means the front door. (b) Beat 16 packs about six events into one beat (the slam, windows lighting, the neighbour, the grip opening, 00:00, blue light and arrest). At Shorts pace that's busy, but it reads as a climax montage.
- fix: Optional. Make it "Do not open *her* door." In beat 16, give the shot of the watch at 00:00 its own half-second.
### viewer participation
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8
- evidence: The viewer gets checkable evidence that cuts both ways, very much the broken-headlight kind. For "he belongs": the licence beside the brass 9 and the MARSH bell label (beat 3), Biscuit's tail thumping at his voice (beat 5), and the drawing of DADDY in a green coat, with the green coat on the man in front of us (beats 2 and 5). For "something's wrong": cash with "no call to the office", the finger to his lips while he glances at the neighbours' windows (beat 2), "She won't come down" (beat 4), and a bright new cylinder in old paint with fresh filings on the mat (beat 6). Then the strip of light under the curtain goes out (beat 7). An attentive viewer can catch R. Marsh against D. Marsh on their own. From beat 9 on, the participation becomes "what would I do?": stay in the locked van as told, or go back out.
- problem: none
- fix: none
### cause and effect
- result: pass
- beats: 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16
- evidence: Each beat follows from the one before. The reveal (beat 8) leads to the excuse and the retreat to the van (9) and the 999 call (10). The van means he's no longer at the door, so Marsh goes to the window with a brick (11). That brick makes the decision (12): "He still thinks I'm his way in. While he does, he'll wait at the door." Then "Found them!" puts the brick down (13). The fake picking buys 50 seconds and is caught (14: "You're not even trying."). The ultimatum forces the open refusal (15). The refusal, shouted down a street that Marsh wanted kept asleep (planted in beat 2, the finger to his lips aimed at the neighbours), wakes the windows, and witnesses make him let go and back off (16). The police time was bought by the stall. **Phase-1 I1:** no longer met. The jammed lock is gone. The climax is the protagonist's refusal turned against the antagonist's need for quiet, which was shown in beat 2, and the four minutes are *earned* by his stall, not just waited out. The brick isn't forgotten: it's lifted, dropped and looked at (beats 11, 13, 14), and he walks past it because the street is now awake.
- problem: The one remaining wrinkle, the horn logic in beat 11 against the shout in beat 15, is a logic gap rather than a broken chain. See the plot hole test.
- fix: See the plot hole test.
### emotional investment

### visual communication
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 6, 8, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18
- evidence: Mute test: the discoveries are all readable inserts (job card, visor letter, licence beside the brass 9, drawing, macro of the new cylinder and filings, the History screen, REFUSED BY TECHNICIAN). The actions are physical and clear: the key bumping at the keyway, the light going out, the brick weighed at the glass, a hand over the horn and then pulled away, the phone slid into the pocket, the pick lying *beside* the keyway rather than in it, the hand on the shoulder, window after window lighting, blue light. What's lost is the operator's information and the final line.
- problem: Minor staging issues, not blocking. (a) Beat 12, the decision beat, is described as "my face in the phone's light, deciding" with a "pov (hands in frame)" camera. In POV we can't see his face, so as boarded the decision has no visible face. It still reads through the hands (phone into pocket, cuff over the watch, hand on the handle). (b) Beat 15: in POV the shout is pure sound. Muted, the viewer sees him stand, then cuts to a wide of a still-dark street. The cause of the lights is only heard.
- fix: Optional. Beat 12: show his face as a reflection in the dark windscreen or the phone glass, or drop "my face" and let the hands carry it. Beat 15: in the wide down the street, let the first window light up *during* the shout, not in the next beat, so the effect sits on the cause.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes. The obstacles are physical (a lock, a window, a brick) and the antagonist has obvious alternatives.
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16
- finding: **Why doesn't Marsh just break the window from the start?** It's answered on screen: he wants it quiet (beat 2), and a locksmith entry to his own address looks legitimate. That's why he reaches for the brick only once his way in walks off (beat 11) and drops it the moment the locksmith returns (13). **Why doesn't the locksmith just drive off, or honk from the locked van?** Beat 11 answers it: the brick is already at the glass. **Phase-1 I2:** (1) The app. Mostly gone. The record now says only "Keys issued to R. Marsh only", not "do not open", so the app booking the job is no contradiction. The narrator not checking History before the ID is now the *theme* of the last line rather than a gap. (2) The phone. Fixed: the screen is turned away in beat 8, and in beat 12 it's in his breast pocket face-in. (3) The horn. It's now weighed on screen, but that answer creates the remaining gap. Beat 11 says "A horn, and he's through that glass before anyone's out of bed". Four beats later, the protagonist deliberately wakes the street with a shout, and Marsh backs off instead of going through the glass. The picture does give a reason: the brick is on the ground, Marsh's hands are on the locksmith, and only about 01:40 is left. But the story never shows the trade the narrator is making, his body between Marsh and the window, so the reason has to be inferred. (4) "No invoice, no call to the office": fixed. It now reads as a red flag ("don't check with your office"), not as a contradiction of the app. **A smaller convenience:** "four minutes out" lands on exactly 00:00. It's acceptable, and set up. **Top-comment probe:** the most-liked critical comment will be "So honking would make him smash the window, but screaming in his face wouldn't?" The runner-up: "Operator: stay in your vehicle. Him: anyway". **Fix:** in beat 15, stage his rise so that he ends up on the path between Marsh and the brick and the bay window (the wide from the doorstep can show it), and glance at the cuffed watch just before he shouts. Then the picture says: now there's someone between him and the glass, and the police are seconds away.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes. The hook ("paying me double to break into his own house") grips at once, and a new clue arrives every beat or two. The peaks are the dog's tail and the DADDY drawing (beat 5), the hands going still on the new cylinder (6), and the strip of light going out upstairs (7), which is the chill most people will mention. Next come the brick at the glass with a hand over the horn (11) and him walking back out against the operator's order (12–13). The main swipe risk is beats 3–4 (ID check, ringing the bell), about five seconds of procedure, held only by "the wrong man twice". A smaller one is the busy beat 16. A likely top comment: "'I checked who he was. I never asked who the lock was for.' Chills." (or "the light going off upstairs got me"). The top critical comment: "So honking would make him smash the window, but screaming in his face wouldn't?", then "operator: stay in your vehicle. him: anyway". It's shareable: a fresh POV (the tradesman asked to open the door), moral clarity without a speech, and a bittersweet sting ("REVIEW REQUIRED").
- verdict: ready
- top-fixes: 1. Beat 15: stage the rise so he ends up on the path between Marsh and the brick and bay window, and glance at the cuffed watch (about 01:40) just before the shout. Then the picture answers "why not the horn?": now there's a body between Marsh and the glass, and the police are seconds away.
