### visual communication
- result: weak
- beats: 8, 11, 1, 6
- evidence: Muted, most of it reads: the counters tick on inserts (beats 1, 6, 9, 10), the pointing and laughter in beat 5, Milo's soundless mouth in beat 8. Beat 8, though, is one silent beat with about six separate actions: he grabs a man and mimes "this tall, yellow coat", the man taps his counter (1), the man pulls his daughter close, Milo pats his pockets for a pen, he tries to shout and only air comes out, and a yellow raincoat drifts towards the gate. In beat 11, the son "signs thank you to Milo", which reads as the son thanking him, not teaching him. The counters are small wrist objects and are only legible on the inserts.
- problem: At Shorts pace, beat 8 will blur: the viewer misses either the "1" on the man's counter or the yellow coat drifting off, and those are the two things that matter. A misread beat 11 muddies the moral just before the ending.
- fix: Split beat 8 into two shots: (a) the mime and the man's counter, "1", on an insert; (b) the silent shout, then a POV of the yellow coat at the gate. Cut the pocket-patting, or reduce it to one pat. In beat 11, have the son take Milo's hand and shape the sign, chin outwards, so it plainly reads as "here's how".
### originality
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 10
- evidence: The skeleton of the first two thirds is *In This Town, You Get 1,000,000 Steps*: a one-line rule with a body-worn countdown and a hard consequence at zero (beat 1); "proof" vignettes of how life adapts (beat 2, picture menus and signing, like wagons and zip lines); a protagonist who squandered his allowance for fun (beat 3); then an emergency in which a small child drifts towards traffic while a bystander holds back because of the rule (beats 7, 8, 10, like the stroller rolling to the river with frozen bystanders). The premise's exact nouns, a limit of 100 words a day and a counter on the wrist, are also those of Christina Dalcher's novel *Vox*, which commenters are likely to name. The second half is genuinely different: the rescue comes from the sacrifice of the person he mocked, not from his own loophole, and the ending is gratitude, not a ripple.
- problem: The benchmarks file says the previous "100 Words" draft matched the Steps skeleton almost beat for beat. This version still opens on the same scaffold and keeps a near-swap of the "stroller and river" emergency. The comparison will be made in the first five seconds, which is exactly where the viewer decides to stay.
- fix: Keep the second half and change the emergency so it's no longer "small child near a road with a vehicle": for example, the exit crowd carries Mia onto the wrong departing fair bus, and "Yellow coat! Stop!" is shouted at its driver. Also change the premise's surface away from *Vox*: a different number, and a counter that isn't a wrist display (for example, beads on a cord that go dark, one per word).

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the whole climax rests on Milo being unable to raise an alarm
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 8, 9, 10
- finding: The rule takes away the *voice* (beat 1: "your voice stops"). It doesn't take away clapping, whistling, banging something or simply running after the coat, and the top comment will be "just whistle" or "why didn't he run after her?" The man in beat 8 has 1 word but could still point, follow or wave. As written, he refuses by tapping his counter, which looks like an excuse, not an inability. The woman's counter reads 3 at the morning bus stop (beat 2), so she has been at 3 since early morning: an unexplained oddity that viewers may pick at ("how is she at 3 before 9 a.m.?"). The core logic holds, since nobody else knows a child is lost and Milo can't tell them. Fix: add one shot in beat 8 of Milo clapping and waving, swallowed by the fair music and the crowd surge, and have the beat 8 man look where Milo points, shrug and see nothing.
### consistency

- result: pass

### originality
- applies: yes
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 10
- finding: The closest benchmark is *In This Town, You Get 1,000,000 Steps* (plus *Vox* for the premise's exact surface). Three structural differences: (1) the hero fails outright and is rescued by others' sacrifice, not by discovering his own loophole; (2) the rescuer is someone he wronged earlier, which gives a moral reversal the benchmark lacks; (3) the ending is a personal act of gratitude and restraint, not the town copying him. From the "Don't copy" column it reuses "limited steps" as limited words (a noun swap) and a close cousin of "the stroller and river" (a small child drifting towards a road and a bus). It avoids the handstand loophole and the ripple ending. More than the nouns differ, but the opening and the emergency are recognisably the same. The protagonist's name, Milo, is also reused from Float Away.

### cold viewer
- applies: yes
- result: pass
- beats: 1-12
- finding: "In a world where everyone gets 100 words a day, a boy who wastes his on jokes mocks a mum for having three left, then blows his last ten at a fair and can't call out when his little sister wanders towards the road. The mum spends her three to save her, and the next morning he spends his first two thanking her in sign and saves the rest for his sister." ## Overall
- keep-watching: yes, the first line is a clear rule with a visible counter, the counting invites participation, and the mocked woman's sacrifice is a strong emotional turn; a viewer who knows the Steps short may leave at beat 2–3 when they recognise the scaffold, and others may stumble at the overloaded beat 8.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Originality: replace the "small child drifting towards traffic" emergency (a near-swap of Steps' stroller and river), for example with Mia carried onto the wrong departing fair bus by the exit crowd and the woman shouting "Yellow coat! Stop!" to its driver, and move the premise's surface away from *Vox* (a different number, and a counter that isn't a wrist display). 2. Beat 11: have the son physically shape the thank-you sign with Milo's hand so it reads as teaching, not thanking; in beat 12 cut "He signed it too".
