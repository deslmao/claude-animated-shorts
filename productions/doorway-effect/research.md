# Why you forget why you walked into a room: research

## Summary
Walking through a doorway can make you forget what you were about to do (the "doorway effect", or location updating
effect). In Gabriel Radvansky's experiments, people who carried objects through a doorway remembered them worse than
people who walked the same distance within one room, in virtual and real rooms. The explanation is the event
horizon model: the brain segments experience into events, and a doorway is an event boundary that "files away" the
previous episode, making what belonged to it harder to retrieve. The effect is not universal: a 2021 series of
experiments (McFadyen et al.) found no overall doorway effect and an impairment only when participants were also
under working-memory load.

## Angles and visual opportunities
- Hook: you walk into a room and forget why; scientists think the doorway did it.
- Analogy: the brain cuts life into scenes like a film editor; a doorway is a cut, and the old scene is filed away.
- Visuals: Milo's thought (keys) as a bubble that pops on the threshold; the old room "filed" as a card or folder; a
  point-of-view walk through the door; the keys as the object that carries the memory; the walk back.

## Claims NOT used (unverified, exaggerated or disputed)
- "Doorways erase memories": not claimed. The effect makes retrieval harder, and only in some conditions.
- "Going back always restores the memory": not claimed. In Radvansky's 2011 loop experiment (several doorways leading
  back to the starting room), returning to the original room did not restore memory, which pointed at the doorway
  itself rather than the room as the cause. The script only says going back "can help" through the old room's cues,
  and shows it as Milo's story, not as a study result.
- Exact sample sizes and effect sizes are not quoted.
