## Criteria
### clarity
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 4, 5, 8, 10, 11
- evidence: Beat 1 sets who, where, when and what's odd in one line ("At two-fourteen in the morning, a man is paying me double to break into his own house"), and the visor letter sets the narrator's stake. Beat 4's "She won't come down" brings in an unnamed "she", and beat 5's drawing (MUMMY, ME, BISCUIT, DADDY in a green coat) identifies her straight away. Beat 8 turns the History text into plain words: "She changed the lock yesterday. And kept the keys from him." That tells a viewer who can't read "R. Marsh" against "D. MARSH" at speed who is who. Beats 10 and 11 make it clear how the operator knows things: "She called us too… She's still on the line upstairs. She says he's trying the windows." She's on a separate 999 call to the same control room, which is easy to accept.
- problem: Minor. Beat 1 packs three things into one beat (the windscreen, the job card, a 15-word warning letter). The letter only explains itself in beat 3. Also, "I can't afford to turn away the wrong man twice" (beat 3) can briefly read as "the wrong man = the dangerous one" before the viewer settles on "refuse a genuine customer". Neither breaks understanding.
- fix: (Optional) hold the visor insert long enough to read it, or cut the letter to "FINAL WARNING: WRONGFUL REFUSAL". Consider "I can't afford to wrongly refuse a second time."

### cause and effect
- result: pass
- beats: 2, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17
- evidence: Each of Marsh's moves follows from what he wants and what he believes. He wants entry that is quiet and looks legitimate (2: the finger to his lips, aimed at the neighbours' windows). When the locksmith leaves, he goes to the window his eyes had already found (9 → 11). He drops the brick and comes back the moment the quiet route seems to work again ("Found them!", 13). When he sees the picking is fake, he moves to a threat (14). The narrator's guess, "He still thinks I'm his way in" (12), gets tested on screen and holds. At 01:30 the stall can't last, so the narrator stands between Marsh and the glass and makes the street wake up (15). The neighbour comes out because of the shout and the thud (16). That is a direct consequence, not a convenient rescue, and it pays off the "quiet" setup: with witnesses, Marsh lets go and backs off. The police arrive on the clock that beat 10 started. Phase-1 finding I1 (the climax was a gesture, and the rescue came from the schedule): the viewer no longer meets it. The jammed lock is gone. The protagonist's choice (refuse aloud, block, wake the street) is what stops "Open it now. Or I go through that window" in the 90 seconds before the police can arrive. The "he had a brick the whole time" objection now has an answer on screen (11 → 13).
- problem: Two small residues. (a) It's a coincidence that Marsh books Kestrel, the firm that changed the lock yesterday. It's acceptable because it creates the problem rather than solving it, but it's unexplained. (b) Marsh lets go at roughly 01:00–01:30 on the watch and is still at the end of the path at 00:00. A man who backs off because of witnesses would normally be gone in a minute.
- fix: (Optional) Show the watch nearer 00:15 when his grip opens in beat 16, so the officers catch him as he reaches the road. Optionally, show a Kestrel van sticker or flyer on Marsh's phone in beat 2 ("found you online"), so his choice of firm is motivated.

### visual communication
- result: weak
- beats: 11, 12, 14, 15, 18
- evidence: Most key actions read with the sound off: the brick weighed at the glass and the hand hovering over the horn (11), the brick dropped as he returns (13), the pick resting beside the keyway (14), window after window lighting up with the neighbour on his step (16), the blue light and the officers (17). The decision itself is staged in a way that hides its logic. Beat 15 is "pov (rising, the brick and the bay window behind me, him at the door)". From the narrator's eyes, the brick and the window are behind the camera. A viewer, muted or not, sees only Marsh at his door and a man shouting. The one spatial fact that makes the shout safe where the horn wasn't (I'm now between him and the glass) is never on screen. Beat 12's decision is a reflection in a windscreen, which is close to a static pose. Beat 18's emotional turn is voice through a door, so muted viewers depend on captions.
- problem: Beat 11 told the viewer that noise makes him go through the glass. Without seeing the new blocking, the climax looks as if the narrator does the very thing he just ruled out. This is the most likely critical comment, and it sits on the climax.
- fix: In beat 15, after he rises, cut from pov to one low side-on wide from the pavement: Marsh against his own door, the narrator on the path, the brick and the bay window clearly behind the narrator. Only then make the shout. Alternatively, keep pov but add a quick glance back over his shoulder at the brick and the glass before he turns to shout. One narration fragment would also do it, e.g. "Now I'm between him and the glass."

### cold viewer
