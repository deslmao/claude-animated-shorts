# Independent evaluation: Locked Out
packet: 77e72699

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 3, 8, 10, 15
- evidence: The hook is the whole situation in 17 words ("At two-fourteen in the morning, a man is paying me double to break into his own house"), and from beat 4 to the end each line adds one clear fact: "She won't come down", "New locks don't just stop working", "It's one of ours", "She changed the lock yesterday. And kept the keys from him", "Officers are four minutes out", "Or I go through that window". Who, where, what and what's at stake are almost always clear. The weak point is the narrator's own stake. It lives in a three-sentence visor letter that no line reads aloud (beat 1, an insert shown alongside the job card), and then in one line, "And I can't afford to turn away the wrong man twice" (beat 3). In a thriller, "the wrong man" means the dangerous man, so heard once at speed the line seems to say the opposite of what it means. The irony only lands for someone who has already read the letter. Beat 2 adds a small muddle: "No invoice, no call to the office" is said about a job that is already on the firm's app (the LOCKOUT job card, beat 1). Phase-1 I3(1), the lie "at her mother's": gone. Every line Marsh speaks is now true, so "Everything he's told me is true" (beat 9) holds.
- problem: The viewer may miss why the narrator accepts a man the audience already suspects, and that reason is what later pays off in "I thought you were going to let him in" (beat 18) and in the final line. Beat 1 asks the viewer to read two inserts at once, the job card and the letter, while the hook line plays.
- fix: In beat 3 replace the line with a plain one, for example "One more refused job and I'm fired." Hold the visor letter long enough to read (about 1.5 s), or trim it to two lines ("FINAL WARNING. ONE MORE WRONGFUL REFUSAL: DISMISSED"). Also see the plot-hole fix for "no call to the office".

### visual communication
- result: weak
- beats: 11, 12, 15, 16
- evidence: Muted, nearly everything reads: the cash, the finger to the lips, the licence beside the brass 9, the new cylinder macro, the light going out, the History screen, the brick at the glass, the hand over the horn, the phone into the pocket, the brick dropped, the pick beside the keyway, the window lighting during the shout, the grip opening, the blue light, the hand stopping at the letterbox, the family at the window. But the one spatial fact that makes the climax work is told, not shown. Beat 15's camera is "pov (rising, the brick and the bay window behind me, him at the door)": a point-of-view shot looking at Marsh cannot show what is behind the narrator, and the next shot is a wide down the street. So "Now I'm between him and that window" exists only in narration. That fact is exactly why a shout works here when a horn would not have (beat 11: from the van, the noise starts a race to the glass that he wins; here the narrator is the obstacle). Phase-1 I2(2), the live phone in plain view: resolved, since the call is in the breast pocket, screen inward (beat 12), and the History is angled away (beat 8).
- problem: The climax's logic rests on geometry the picture never establishes. Without it, sharp viewers will say "you just said making noise makes him smash in", and muted viewers can't follow it at all.
- fix: In beat 15, before the shout, add one establishing shot, a low-angle wide or a side angle about 20 to 30° above horizontal, of door, Marsh, narrator, then the brick on the path and the bay window behind him. Or turn the point of view: look back at the brick and the bay window, then forward to Marsh pinned against his own door. Then the line "Now I'm between him and that window" can be shortened or cut.

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the story turns on obstacles (the locked door, the horn, the window) and on how a firm's system handled the job.
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 2, 8, 11, 12, 15
- finding: The phase-1 holes, item by item. I2(1), the app saying "DO NOT OPEN" and still sending him: much reduced. The History now records only a lock change with keys issued to R. Marsh, with no instruction, and the last line owns the failure ("I never asked who the lock was for"). But a viewer still meets a smaller version: his own firm changed this lock for her yesterday, and the same firm's app booked a 2 a.m. lockout for a different Marsh at that address without flagging it. I2(2), the phone in view: resolved (beat 12). I2(3), the horn: resolved, weighed on screen and explained (beat 11). I2(4), no paperwork for an app job: still present in new words, "No invoice, no call to the office" (beat 2) over an app job card (beat 1). I2(5), the jammed lock: gone. Remaining smaller points: the same firm on both nights is convenient but plausible for a local firm. Why does Marsh want a locksmith rather than the brick? He wants it quiet, so it looks lawful, and beat 2 makes that clear. Top-comment probe: "Your own company changed that lock yesterday and the app still sent you there at 2am?" A second would be "Same locksmith both nights? Convenient." Fix: make the job a direct call to the number on the van, not an app booking. Beat 1's phone shows an incoming call or a text ("LOCKED OUT, 9 ALDER ROW, PAYING DOUBLE"), and beat 2's "no call to the office" then follows naturally: he is keeping it off the firm's books, which is also why nothing flagged it.

### redundancy
- applies: yes, the story is narrated over planned shots.
- result: weak
- beats: 8, 11, 15, 16
- finding: Beat 16's "One light. Then another. Then the whole street." describes exactly what the picture and sound already deliver at the climax's peak. Cut it, and let the lights, the doors and the neighbour's "Oi!" carry it. Beat 15 is the densest beat (three narration lines plus a 12-word shout). Once the geometry is shown (see visual communication), "Now I'm between him and that window" can be cut, leaving "He wants this quiet. So I make it loud." and the shout. The shout itself is not redundant: it tells the street what is happening, which a horn could not. Beat 8's "She changed the lock yesterday…" and beat 11's horn line add meaning (see the flags). Beat 11's operator "he's trying the windows" overlaps the shot but establishes that she is still watching and on the line, which is fine. Nothing else over-explains. Beat 12's two strategy sentences are earned at once by beat 13.

## Overall

The new lock against "Just stopped working tonight" (beat 6) and "It's one of ours" (beat 8) are strong lean-in moments, and from beat 10 the 04:00 countdown, the brick at the glass and the hand over the horn keep viewers to the end. The swipe risk is beats 3 to 5: about ten seconds of procedure (licence, bell, letterbox) with the clunky "wrong man twice" line, before the lock beat pays it off. A faster beat 3 protects it. Top comment: "'He wants this quiet. So I make it loud.' Chills. And the kid's drawing with Daddy in it, everything he said was true." Top critical comment: "Your own company changed that lock yesterday and the app still sent you there at 2am?"
