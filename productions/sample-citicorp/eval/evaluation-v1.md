# Independent evaluation: The Skyscraper Fixed in Secret
packet: 5d484b7e

## Criteria
### clarity
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 4, 5, 7
- evidence: The spoken lines never say who the protagonist is. Beat 2 is about the tower ("The tower was strange... a huge concrete block slid to calm the sway"), and the man rocking the model is named only in the storyboard. Beat 4 then says "a student called his office", where "his" has no antecedent, and the picture shows a junior engineer answering. Beat 5 opens "He checked", straight after that junior engineer is on screen. Beat 7's "Telling them could end his career" depends on knowing that he designed the tower, which is never said. Beat 2 also packs three new things into one beat: the church on the corner, legs at the middle of each side, and a sliding block on top.
- problem: A first-time viewer has to work out that the man is the tower's own engineer, and may take "He checked" to mean the junior engineer. Without that, the career risk and the confession in beat 7 (the moral centre of the film) lose their weight. Beat 2 is the densest beat, it comes right after the hook, and it's where attention is most fragile.
- fix: In beat 2, name him in narration as the designer: "Its engineer, William LeMessurier, gave it a strange design: a church owned the corner, so..." Change beat 5 to "LeMessurier checked." Move the damper line into beat 3, or give it its own short beat, so beat 2 carries only the legs.

### viewer participation
- result: weak
- beats: 7, 9, 10
- evidence: Beat 7 sets up the dilemma and settles it in the same beat: "Nobody else had noticed. Telling them could end his career. / He told them anyway." Beat 9 brings in the hurricane, and the first line of beat 10 settles it: "It turned out to sea."
- problem: The two moments where a viewer could ask "what would I do?" or "will it hold?" are closed before the viewer can engage. Everything else is told, not weighed. The film's best question (stay silent and probably get away with it, or confess) gets about two seconds.
- fix: Split beat 7. End one beat on "Nobody else knew. Telling them could end his career.", hold on him with the papers in hand for a silent second, then cut to "He told them anyway." as the papers slide. In beats 9–10, show half the joints still bare (the counter stopped partway) as the spiral approaches, before it turns.

### cause and effect
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10
- evidence: The fan on the face (3), then the question about the corner (4), then the fan on the corner and red braces, made worse by the bolts (5), then the 16-year risk (6), then the confession (7), then the night repairs (8), the evacuation plan and the hurricane (9), and completion and secrecy (10). Each beat follows from the one before it.
- problem: A minor point: the hurricane turns away by luck, and the protagonist's only action at the peak of the tension is a clenched fist. It's history, and the resolution is still the repairs his choice set in motion, so the chain holds.
- fix: n/a

### emotional investment
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 6, 7, 9
- evidence: The only personal stake is one stated line in beat 7, "Telling them could end his career." The human danger is abstract: a calendar strip and a damper grinding to a halt (6), and a ring on a map (9). The office workers with coffee in beat 1 are never linked back to the danger. LeMessurier has no voice, no line and no personal detail. His feelings are shown only as "staring at the number" and a "fist clenched".
- problem: The viewer understands the stakes but doesn't feel them before the climax. Nobody we've seen is shown to be at risk, and the career risk is asserted, not dramatised.
- fix: In beat 6, as the damper stops, cut back to beat 1's coffee-carrying office workers (or the street and church beneath the overhang) so "topple" has faces. In the split beat 7, give the hesitation a visible action (his hand pauses on the papers) before he pushes them across.

- problem: Beat 11's reveal risks deflating the story more than deepening it (see cold viewer), but it doesn't contradict anything shown.

### visual communication
- result: weak
- beats: 5, 6, 9, 10, 11
- evidence: Several key ideas are carried by abstract or text-based pictures. In beat 5, a contractor's note reading "welds changed to bolts" has to be read in a quick insert. In beat 6, a "calendar strip of sixteen years with a storm icon" doesn't read muted as "could topple once every 16 years". Beat 9 crowds an evacuation map, a weather map and a TV-watching reaction into one beat. In beat 10, an empty newsstand with a STRIKE sign doesn't read as "the story stayed secret". In beat 11, "two wind diagrams side by side" and an engineer who "frowns, then nods" are unreadable without the narration. The failure the whole story is about (the tower coming down) is never shown, even as a simulation on the model.
- problem: Muted, the second half reads as a string of maps and diagrams. The key danger and the twist ending depend entirely on narration.
- fix: In beat 5, show the swap physically: a welded joint on the model dissolves into a bolted one, with no readable note needed. In beat 6, have the model tower lean and crack at a red joint as the block stops, then show the "16 years" figure. Split beat 9 into the map and then the TV forecast. In beat 11, use the model and the fan again: head-on gives a deeper red than the corner. Don't introduce a new diagram format in the last beat.

### originality
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 11
- evidence: This is a factual retelling of a well-known case. It differs from the standard telling (Morgenstern's chronological account) by opening in medias res on the hidden welders (beat 1), explaining the mechanism with a model and a fan (beats 3, 5), and closing on the NIST reassessment (beat 11). It isn't another story with the nouns swapped.
- problem: The subject is heavily covered online. The cold open and the epilogue are the differentiators, so the epilogue needs to land clearly (see visual communication and cold viewer).
- fix: n/a

### accuracy
- result: weak
- beats: 4, 5, 7, 10, 11
- evidence: (a) Beat 5: "He checked... Then he learned something worse. To save money, the welded joints had been changed to bolts." In the standard account (Morgenstern, via Online Ethics Center and Wikipedia), LeMessurier learned of the bolt substitution in May 1978, during a Pittsburgh project meeting, about a month *before* the student's call. The call then made him rerun the corner-wind loads on the bolted joints. The script reverses the order and invents a contractor's note as the way he found out. The writer's own research ("a change LeMessurier learned of afterwards") is ambiguous on this and doesn't support the order shown. (b) Beat 10: "a newspaper strike kept the story quiet for years." The 1978 strike lasted months. It helped keep the story out of the press *at the time*, and the story then stayed largely unknown until 1995 for other reasons. The research says the strike "helped keep the story out of the press", not that it kept it quiet for years. (c) Beat 7's "Nobody else had noticed" sits awkwardly with beat 4, where a student raised exactly the corner-wind question. Accurately, nobody else had put the corner winds and the bolts together. (d) The caller's identity is disputed (one 2022 account names a different student), and the script handles that well by keeping the caller unnamed in narration. The 16-year figure, the roughly 200 plates, the evacuation plans, Ella and the NIST finding are all worded in line with the research.
- problem: The dramatisation changes the order of what happened, and one causal claim (the strike) is overstated. For a factual Short, both are avoidable errors that a knowledgeable commenter would point out.
- fix: Beat 5: "He already knew something worrying: to save money, his welded joints had been swapped for bolts. Now he ran the corner winds through the bolts." Drop the contractor's note, or show it as an earlier memory. Beat 10: "...and a newspaper strike kept it out of the papers. The public didn't hear about it until 1995." Beat 7: "Nobody else had put it together."

## Stress tests
### plot hole
- applies: yes, the obstacle is a tower that could topple, and the response is secret night repairs while it stays occupied
- result: weak
- beats: 1, 6, 8, 9
- finding: A cold viewer will ask why, if it could topple in a storm (6), office workers are strolling through it with coffee every morning (1), and why the building wasn't simply closed. The script answers only with an evacuation plan for the surrounding blocks (9). The obvious simpler fix for beat 6's stated trigger (a power failure stopping the damper) is backup power, which was in fact arranged (emergency generators for the damper were part of the precautions). The story is silent on it, so the risk seems to go unmanaged. A short clause would close it, e.g. in beat 9: "They kept the damper on backup power, watched the weather hour by hour, and quietly planned to evacuate..."

### consistency
- applies: yes, several references back to earlier beats
- result: weak
- beats: 4, 7, 10
- finding: "Nobody else had noticed" (7) contradicts beat 4, where a student asks exactly "What happens when the wind hits a corner?". "Every night" (8) matches "all night" and "by morning they were gone" (1). "With the work half done" (9) is consistent with the counter in 8, if the counter is shown stopping partway. "That autumn" (10) fits a September hurricane. "Kept the story quiet for years" (10) attributes years of silence to the strike, which isn't consistent with the research.

### giveaway
- applies: yes, automated flag on beats 5 and 8
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 5, 8
- finding: Beat 5 names the defect (bolts instead of welds), not the remedy. The steel plates are first shown in beat 8. Beat 1's secret welders plant the solution as a mystery without explaining it, which is good planting. Nothing announces the fix early.

### dilemma balance
- applies: no, there's no on-screen choice and no competing explanations for the viewer to weigh; the story ends on its last beat
- result: pass
- beats: 7
- finding: Not applicable. The only dilemma (confess or stay silent) belongs to the protagonist and is settled in beat 7.

### redundancy
- applies: yes, the narration often sits over illustrative shots
- result: pass
- beats: 3, 8, 10
- finding: Minor overlaps. In beat 8, "Around two hundred of them" doubles the counter ticking to 200. In beat 10, "It turned out to sea" doubles the bending spiral. Beat 3's line describes the glowing braces but adds the mechanism (wind carried into the legs), so it earns its place. None is long enough to hurt pacing, but "Around two hundred of them" could be cut in favour of the counter.

### originality
- applies: yes, a factual retelling of a famous engineering case
- result: pass
- beats: 1, 3, 5, 11
- finding: The closest well-known telling is Joe Morgenstern's 1995 New Yorker piece "The Fifty-Nine-Story Crisis" and its many retellings (for example 99% Invisible's "Structural Integrity"). The structural differences: (1) it opens in medias res on the hidden welders, not with the design or the phone call; (2) it explains the mechanism with a physical model and a fan, face versus corner; (3) it ends on the 2019–2020 NIST reassessment rather than on the repairs or the 1995 disclosure. It's the same true story, but not just the same telling.

### emotional cost
- applies: yes, the protagonist has a personal and a public stake
- result: weak
- beats: 6, 7, 9
- finding: If he stays silent and a storm comes, the tower falls on its occupants and neighbours. If he speaks, he risks his career. The career risk is stated once (7). The human cost is implied only through abstractions (a 16-year strip in 6, a map ring in 9). Neither is shown happening to anyone the viewer has seen before the hurricane climax.

### cold viewer
- applies: yes, always
- result: weak
- beats: 2, 4, 5, 7, 11
- finding: A cold viewer would think: "In 1978 an engineer found that a strange new New York tower on stilts could blow over in a corner wind, made worse because bolts had replaced welds. He owned up, and the tower was secretly reinforced at night while a hurricane nearly hit. Decades later, someone worked out that the corner winds weren't actually the main danger." Likely misunderstandings: (a) not knowing that the man is the tower's own designer, which blunts "could end his career"; (b) thinking the junior engineer who took the call is the one who "checked"; (c) being puzzled by "Nobody else had noticed" after a student asked exactly that; (d) reading "once every sixteen years" as "it will fall within sixteen years"; (e) leaving on beat 11 thinking the whole scare was a mistake and the building was never in danger, which deflates the climax. The final line, "with what he knew, he told the truth", partly recovers this, but the diagram doesn't show whether the bolted tower was ever safe.

## Overall
- keep-watching: yes, the hook (secret welders vanishing behind plywood by morning) is strong and pulls through. The main drop-off risk is beats 2–3, a dense architecture lesson right after the hook. A smaller risk is a flat finish at beat 11, where an unreadable diagram undercuts the climax.
- verdict: revise
- top-fixes: 1. Identify the protagonist and fix the story logic: name LeMessurier in beat 2 as the tower's own engineer, make beat 5 "LeMessurier checked", and replace "Nobody else had noticed" with "Nobody else had put it together". 2. Correct the facts: he already knew about the bolts (May 1978) before the call, so reword beat 5 as "He already knew... now he ran the corner winds through the bolts" and drop the invented contractor's note; reword beat 10 so the strike kept the story out of the papers at the time and it stayed unknown until 1995. 3. Make the stakes and the dilemma felt and visible: in beat 6 show the model failing at a red joint and cut back to beat 1's coffee-carrying workers, split beat 7 so "could end his career" holds a moment before "He told them anyway", and in beat 11 reuse the model and fan (head-on redder than corner) instead of a new diagram, with a line making clear nobody could know that in 1978.
